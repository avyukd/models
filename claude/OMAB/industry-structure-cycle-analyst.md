# Mexican Airport Industry: Structure and Cycle Analysis

## Executive Summary

The Mexican airport industry is a regulated oligopoly operating under 50-year concessions (1998-2048) with renewal rights to 2098. Three private groups (ASUR, GAP, OMA) control 35 airports handling 90%+ of national traffic. The industry exhibits moderate cyclicality tied to Mexico-U.S. economic linkages, with current conditions best described as **mid-cycle recovery from a temporary supply shock** (Pratt & Whitney engine recalls) rather than demand weakness.

**The lazy consensus view** is that Mexican airports are a straightforward play on nearshoring and Mexico macro. That view is incomplete. The real story is about **regulatory risk asymmetry**, **airline industry consolidation creating customer concentration**, and **a supply-constrained period masking underlying demand strength**.

## Industry Structure

### Market Configuration: Regulated Regional Monopolies

The Mexican government privatized airports in 1998, creating three regional monopolies:

| Operator | Key Assets | Traffic Share | Revenue Mix |
|----------|-----------|---------------|-------------|
| **ASUR** | Cancun (57% of revenue), Puerto Rico (15%), Colombia (10%) | ~54% of private group traffic | Tourism-heavy, 45%+ international |
| **GAP** | Guadalajara, Tijuana, Los Cabos, Puerto Vallarta | ~42% of private group traffic | Balanced domestic/tourism |
| **OMA** | Monterrey (42% of revenue), Ciudad Juarez, Culiacan | ~22% of private group traffic | 87% domestic, industrial/business |

**Critical insight**: These are not competitors. Each operates exclusive regional concessions. The only meaningful competition is from: (1) Mexico City's government-operated AICM, (2) the new government-built AIFA, and (3) ground transportation alternatives.

### Profitability Dynamics

Industry EBITDA margins have historically ranged 60-70%, spiking to 77% during COVID (cost-cutting + regulatory relief). Current margins sit around 60-74% depending on operator:

- **OMA**: 74.4% adjusted EBITDA margin (Q1 2025) - highest in industry
- **ASUR**: ~65% EBITDA margin, but highest absolute profitability
- **GAP**: ~67% EBITDA margin, strongest revenue growth

These margins are extraordinarily high by global airport standards and reflect: (1) limited competition within concession territories, (2) dual-till regulatory structure that leaves commercial revenues unregulated, (3) cost structures denominated in pesos while international fees are USD-linked.

### Revenue Composition

The "dual till" regulatory framework is crucial:

- **Aeronautical revenues** (TUA, landing fees, space rentals): Subject to price caps set every 5 years via Master Development Programs (MDPs)
- **Commercial revenues** (retail, parking, advertising, cargo): Unregulated

This creates a regulatory arbitrage where operators can offset TUA compression by growing commercial revenue per passenger. OMA earns ~Ps. 300/passenger; ASUR ~Ps. 240/passenger. Commercial revenue growth has consistently outpaced aeronautical revenue growth.

## Regulatory Framework: The Real Risk Factor

### Concession Structure

- **Initial term**: 50 years (1998-2048)
- **Renewal option**: Up to additional 50 years (potentially to 2098)
- **MDP cycles**: 5-year investment/tariff negotiations with AFAC

The government can theoretically revoke concessions but has never done so. More practically, it can adjust the economic terms via tariff formula changes.

### October 2023: The Regulatory Wake-Up Call

In October 2023, the Lopez Obrador administration unilaterally modified concession fee structures. Airport stocks fell 40%+ intraday - the worst single-day decline in their history.

**What actually happened**: AFAC changed the formula for maximum tariffs (TUA and related fees). Initial fears of 5-8% TUA cuts and potential 30% EBITDA compression proved overblown. Operators ultimately accepted modified terms, and impacts were manageable.

**What this revealed**: The government has both the legal authority and political willingness to modify economic terms mid-concession. The "unilateral and without prior communication" nature of the change was the real shock.

### AIFA: Government-Created Competition

The government built Felipe Angeles International Airport (AIFA) and has actively tried to divert traffic from Mexico City's AICM to fill it:
- Reduced AICM hourly operations from 61 to 44 slots
- Mandated cargo relocation to AIFA
- AIFA handled 6.3M passengers in 2024 (vs. AICM's 40M+)

**Reality check**: AIFA received Ps. 704M in government subsidies against Ps. 414M in reported "profit" in H1 2025. It remains a political project, not an economic competitor.

## Cyclicality Assessment: Moderate

### Historical Cycle Behavior

| Period | Trigger | Traffic Impact | Margin Impact | Recovery Time |
|--------|---------|---------------|---------------|---------------|
| 2008-2009 | GFC + H1N1 | -15% to -20% | Margins compressed ~500bps | 2-3 years |
| 2020-2021 | COVID | -50%+ at trough | Margins held due to cost cuts | 2 years |
| 2024 | P&W engine recalls | -5% to -7% (domestic) | Margins stable | Ongoing |

Traffic is GDP-correlated with a beta of approximately 1.5-2.0x (traffic grows faster than GDP in expansions, falls faster in contractions). International traffic to ASUR is more volatile (tourism-driven); OMA's domestic-heavy mix is more tied to Mexican industrial production.

### Supply Elasticity: Very Low Short-Term, Moderate Long-Term

- **Short-term**: Zero. You cannot build a new airport in response to demand.
- **Long-term**: Government can build new airports (AIFA, Tulum), but this takes 5-10 years and requires political will.

This creates the classic regulated utility dynamic: when demand exceeds capacity, operators capture excess returns until the next regulatory reset or government intervention.

## Current Cycle Position: Mid-Cycle Recovery from Supply Shock

### The Pratt & Whitney Situation (The Story Everyone Misses)

The 2024 "demand weakness" narrative is wrong. What actually happened:

- P&W GTF engine recalls grounded 30-40% of Volaris and VivaAerobus fleets
- These two airlines control ~71% of domestic traffic
- Available Seat Kilometers (ASKs) were artificially constrained
- Reported "traffic declines" reflected supply unavailability, not demand weakness

**Timeline for resolution**:
- Per Volaris management: Engine issues extend through 2027
- MRO backlog won't normalize until late 2026
- Airlines using wet leases as temporary capacity bridge

**What this means**: 2024's traffic "decline" is not a demand signal. As engine availability normalizes, pent-up demand will flow through. OMA's 8.5% traffic growth in 2025 vs. ASUR's 0.3% reflects OMA's Monterrey airport benefiting from nearshoring demand that could be served by available aircraft.

### Nearshoring: Real but Overhyped in Consensus

Nearshoring is driving genuine incremental demand, particularly at:
- Monterrey (OMA): 14% traffic growth Q3 2025, direct nearshoring beneficiary
- Guadalajara (GAP): Manufacturing corridor
- Northern border cities (OMA/GAP)

However, consensus overstates nearshoring as a transformational driver:
- Total FDI-related traffic is a small percentage of total passengers
- Nearshoring creates business travel demand (higher yield) but limited leisure volume
- Most manufacturing workers don't fly; executive/technical travel is the incremental demand

**Where nearshoring actually matters**: Cargo. AIFA handled 406,000 tons in 2025. Air cargo is growing faster than passenger traffic, but cargo is a small revenue contributor for OMA/ASUR/GAP.

## Mid-Cycle Definition for This Industry

**"Normal" Mexican airport economics look like**:

- Passenger traffic growth: 4-5% annually (roughly 2x Mexico GDP growth)
- EBITDA margins: 60-65%
- TUA/tariff increases: In line with inflation (NPPI-based adjustments)
- Capacity utilization: 70-80% at key airports
- Commercial revenue per passenger: Growing 2-3% real
- No major regulatory interventions
- Airlines operating at normal capacity

**Current deviation from mid-cycle**:
- Traffic growth distorted by P&W groundings (will mean-revert higher)
- Margins slightly elevated from mid-cycle (74% vs. 60-65% historical average)
- Regulatory risk elevated (October 2023 changed baseline expectations)

## The One Industry Factor That Matters

**Airline industry structure determines airport economics.**

The pending Volaris-VivaAerobus merger (announced December 2025) would create a 70%+ domestic market share carrier. This is the single most important structural shift since privatization.

**Why this matters**:
1. A merged entity has significant countervailing power vs. airports
2. Route rationalization could strand certain airports
3. Financial stress at the combined airline would ripple to airports
4. Regulatory approval is uncertain - Mexico's new CNA antitrust body faces its first major test

**Monitoring signal**: CNA ruling on Volaris-VivaAerobus merger (expected 2026). Approval with conditions = status quo. Rejection = continued airline duopoly pressure on margins.

## What Consensus May Be Missing

### Non-Consensus View #1: Regulatory Risk is Asymmetric and Under-Priced

Consensus treats October 2023 as a resolved one-time event. This is naive.

The Lopez Obrador administration demonstrated willingness to modify concession economics unilaterally. The Sheinbaum administration inherited this precedent. With AIFA requiring ongoing subsidies and political pressure to justify the project, future TUA adjustments skewing economics toward government airports are plausible.

**The asymmetry**: Downside scenarios (tariff cuts, forced traffic diversion, unfavorable MDP terms) are government-controlled. Upside scenarios require government inaction. Regulatory risk is structurally short, not symmetric.

### Non-Consensus View #2: 2024-2025 Traffic is Demand Floor, Not Ceiling

Most analysts modeled 2024's traffic weakness as demand-driven, projecting mid-single-digit growth forward. This misses that traffic was supply-constrained.

If P&W issues resolve by late 2026-2027, and nearshoring continues, traffic could accelerate to 8-10% growth rates - materially above consensus 4-5% forecasts. OMA's 8.5% 2025 growth is a preview.

**Bearish variant**: If the Volaris-VivaAerobus merger fails and one carrier becomes financially distressed, traffic could decline regardless of demand.

### Non-Consensus View #3: OMA's Valuation Discount to ASUR/GAP is Warranted

OMA trades at 16.8x EV/EBITDA vs. GAP at 20.5x and ASUR at 18.9x. Consensus view is OMA is "cheap" due to nearshoring upside.

**Contrarian take**: OMA's discount reflects:
- 87% domestic exposure = higher regulatory risk (TUA is domestic-focused)
- Higher peso exposure = no natural USD hedge that ASUR enjoys
- Smaller, more concentrated portfolio = more binary risk to Monterrey
- Nearshoring is priced in; incremental surprise is limited

OMA is not "cheap" - it's appropriately discounted for structural risk factors.
