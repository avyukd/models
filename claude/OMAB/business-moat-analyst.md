# OMAB (Grupo Aeroportuario del Centro Norte) - Business Quality & Moat Analysis

**Date:** January 2026
**Ticker:** OMAB (NYSE ADR) / OMAB.MX (BMV)

---

## 1. Business Model Summary (Economic, Not Narrative)

**How the company makes money:**
OMA operates 13 international airports in central and northern Mexico under 50-year concessions granted in 1998, expiring in 2048 (renewable for up to 50 additional years). Revenue comes from two regulated/unregulated streams:

- **Aeronautical services (~73% of revenue):** TUA (passenger use fees), landing fees, aircraft parking, passenger screening, and jetway usage. These are rate-regulated by the Mexican government through 5-year Master Development Programs (MDPs).

- **Non-aeronautical services (~27% of revenue):** Parking (28% of commercial), restaurants (20%), car rentals (15%), VIP lounges (11%), retail, advertising, hotel operations (NH Collection at Mexico City T2, Hilton Garden Inn at Monterrey), and industrial park leasing.

**Who pays:**
- Airlines (landing fees, parking)
- Passengers (TUA, embedded in ticket prices)
- Commercial tenants (retailers, restaurants, car rentals)
- Hotel guests

**Fixed vs. variable cost structure:**
Airports are high fixed cost, high operating leverage businesses. Once built, marginal cost per passenger is minimal. This produces 65-75% EBITDA margins when passenger volumes exceed breakeven thresholds. Cost structure is dominated by personnel, maintenance, and mandated infrastructure investments under the MDP.

**Where profits are generated:**
Monterrey Airport alone accounts for ~42% of revenue and ~53% of MDP capital allocation. The company is essentially a bet on Monterrey with 12 smaller supporting airports. Non-aeronautical revenue, being unregulated, offers higher incremental margins and strategic optionality.

## 2. Source(s) of Advantage

### 2.1 Regional Monopoly via Concession (Primary Advantage)

**Mechanism:** Each airport operates as a natural monopoly within its geographic catchment. You cannot build a competing airport next to Monterrey International - the concession is exclusive, and even if you could, the regulatory approval and capital requirements make it economically irrational. Airlines serving Monterrey have no alternative airport serving that metropolitan area.

**Economic evidence:** 65-75% EBITDA margins sustained over a decade. ROIC consistently above 13%, per industry analysis, exceeding cost of capital.

**Replication difficulty:** Impossible within the concession period. A competitor would need: (1) government permission to break the exclusive concession, (2) land near major population centers, (3) billions in capital, (4) decades to build route networks. No rational actor attempts this.

### 2.2 High Switching Costs for Airlines

**Mechanism:** Airlines invest heavily in slot positions, ground infrastructure, maintenance facilities, crew bases, and route network optimization around specific airports. VivaAerobus has an exclusive terminal (Terminal C) at Monterrey. Switching to a hypothetical alternative airport would require abandoning sunk investments and rebuilding from scratch - typically tens of millions of dollars per airline.

**Caveat:** Switching costs exist at the airport level, not at the operator level. Airlines cannot switch Monterrey for Guadalajara (different markets), but the government theoretically could reassign the concession to another operator at expiration. This is a structural lock-in with a 2048 expiration date.

### 2.3 Regulatory Barriers to Entry

**Mechanism:** The 1995 Mexican Airport Law created a closed system. Only 35 commercial airports were privatized, divided among three groups (GAP, ASUR, OMA) plus Mexico City (AICM). New airport construction requires federal approval, environmental clearances, and coordination with existing concession holders. The recent AIFA (Felipe Angeles) airport demonstrates government can build new airports, but also shows the difficulty of attracting airlines without organic demand.

**Limitation:** This barrier protects OMA from private competitors but not from government-owned alternatives. AIFA represents a policy-driven attempt to shift traffic, though it has struggled to gain traction with only 6.3 million passengers in 2024 vs. 40+ million at AICM.

### 2.4 Operating Leverage + Fixed Concession Payments

**Mechanism:** The concession fee (increased from 5% to 9% of gross revenue in October 2023) is a variable cost, but operational costs are largely fixed. Every incremental passenger above breakeven flows through at high margin. This creates significant upside to traffic growth and significant downside risk to traffic declines.

**Evidence:** During COVID, when traffic collapsed, OMA cut costs aggressively and received regulatory relief on tariff adjustments, eventually producing higher-than-normal margins temporarily (which the government later clawed back via the 2023 tariff reset).

## 3. Competitive Landscape

### 3.1 Direct Competitors

**Within Mexico:**
- **GAP (Grupo Aeroportuario del Pacifico):** 31% of Mexican passenger traffic; operates Guadalajara, Tijuana, Los Cabos, Puerto Vallarta
- **ASUR (Grupo Aeroportuario del Sureste):** 24% of traffic; operates Cancun, Cozumel, Merida; diversified into Puerto Rico and Colombia
- **OMA:** 14% of traffic; operates Monterrey and 12 regional/border airports

**Competition type:** These three operators do NOT compete head-to-head for the same passengers. Each has exclusive geographic territories. "Competition" occurs only at the margin for airline capacity allocation and tourist marketing. A tourist choosing Cancun vs. Los Cabos indirectly affects ASUR vs. GAP, but this is tourism competition, not airport competition.

**Government-owned competition:**
- AICM (Mexico City International): Saturated, 40M+ passengers
- AIFA (Felipe Angeles): Government-built alternative to AICM, struggling at 6.3M passengers
- Tulum Airport: New government project affecting ASUR's Cancun (domestic routes)

### 3.2 Airline Concentration Risk

This is where OMA's competitive position becomes more nuanced:

| Airline | Share of OMA Traffic |
|---------|---------------------|
| VivaAerobus | 50% |
| Volaris | 23% |
| Aeromexico | 17% |
| Others | 10% |

The "big three" airlines represent 98% of OMA's passenger traffic and 51.6% of total revenue. VivaAerobus and Volaris together control ~73% of traffic. A proposed merger between these two (announced December 2025) would create a dominant carrier controlling 70-75% of Mexican domestic seats.

**Implication:** While OMA has monopoly power over Monterrey airport, the airlines have oligopoly power over the passengers using that airport. The balance of negotiating leverage is not one-sided. Airlines can negotiate landing fee discounts, demand terminal investments, or threaten route reductions. VivaAerobus's exclusive Terminal C at Monterrey demonstrates airline leverage.

### 3.3 Evidence of Price Competition or Margin Pressure

The October 2023 tariff reset is the clearest evidence that OMA's pricing power is constrained. The government unilaterally amended Annex 7 of the concession agreements, resetting the tariff base and increasing concession fees from 5% to 9% of gross revenue. Stock prices dropped 30-40% overnight.

This was not market-driven price competition - it was regulatory recapture of excess returns. Mexican airports had been earning above-normal profits due to COVID-era relief combined with traffic recovery. The government clawed this back, demonstrating that the ultimate pricing power resides with the regulator, not the operator.

## 4. Customer Behavior & Revenue Quality

### 4.1 Customer Concentration

- **Airlines:** Three airlines = 98% of traffic. VivaAerobus alone = 50%.
- **Airports:** Monterrey = 42% of revenue. High geographic concentration.
- **Traffic type:** 87% domestic, 13% international. Heavy exposure to Mexican economic cycle.

This concentration creates fragility. A VivaAerobus operational issue (e.g., the Pratt & Whitney engine recall that grounded GTF-powered aircraft) directly impacts OMA's earnings. A Monterrey-specific event (natural disaster, cartel violence, economic downturn) would disproportionately affect results.

### 4.2 Revenue Visibility and Stickiness

**Aeronautical (73%):** Revenue is mechanically linked to passenger volumes, which are driven by GDP, tourism, and airline capacity deployment. TUA rates are set by 5-year MDPs, providing medium-term visibility but exposure to regulatory reset every five years.

**Non-aeronautical (27%):** More stable as a percentage of passengers but still volume-dependent. Commercial leases provide some contractual stability. Industrial park revenue (growing 53% in recent quarters) represents genuine diversification.

**Contract length:** MDPs lock in tariff ceilings for 5 years. Concession runs to 2048. Commercial leases vary (typically 3-7 years for major tenants).

### 4.3 Pricing Power vs. Discounting Behavior

OMA does not set prices freely. Maximum TUA rates are established by SICT (Ministry of Infrastructure, Communications and Transportation) based on passenger projections, capital investment commitments, and return parameters. OMA can charge up to the maximum but faces airline pushback if rates exceed perceived value.

In practice, OMA offers incentives to airlines for new route development, co-marketing support, and terminal upgrades (see VivaAerobus Terminal C). This suggests pricing power is shared between operator and airlines, with the government as ultimate arbiter.

## 5. Evidence of Moat in the Financials

### 5.1 Margin Stability Across Cycles

| Year | EBITDA Margin |
|------|---------------|
| 2015 | ~62% |
| 2016 | ~64% |
| 2017 | ~66% |
| 2018 | ~68% |
| 2019 | ~73% |
| 2020 | Depressed (COVID) |
| 2021 | Recovery |
| 2022 | ~74% |
| 2023 | ~74% |
| 2024 | ~68-70% (post-tariff reset) |
| 2025 (TTM) | ~75% |

**Interpretation:** Pre-COVID margin expansion reflects operating leverage as traffic grew. Post-2023 tariff reset, margins compressed but remain exceptionally high by industrial standards. The ability to maintain 68%+ EBITDA margins after a hostile regulatory action demonstrates structural cost advantages.

### 5.2 Return on Invested Capital

Per industry analysis, OMA generates ROIC consistently above 13%, exceeding estimated cost of capital. Among the three Mexican airport groups, OMA reportedly has the highest ROIC with the lowest debt load.

**Why this matters:** High ROIC proves the business earns excess returns on capital deployed. The concession model means most "capital" is land and facilities owned by the government - OMA's invested capital is primarily working capital and contractually mandated improvements. This artificially inflates ROIC calculations but also reflects the favorable economics of operating government-owned infrastructure.

### 5.3 Ability to Raise Prices Without Volume Loss

Limited. TUA increases are regulated. However, OMA has demonstrated ability to grow non-aeronautical revenue per passenger (commercial revenue per pax up 13% in Q1 2025) through better tenant mix, higher parking yields, and premium lounge expansion. This is genuine pricing power in the unregulated segment.

## 6. Fragility Analysis (How the Moat Breaks)

### 6.1 Government/Regulatory Risk (Highest Probability)

The October 2023 tariff reset proves the Mexican government will unilaterally modify concession terms if it believes returns are excessive. There is no legal mechanism to prevent this - Annex 7 of the concession explicitly allows SICT to review tariffs based on legislative changes or traffic fluctuations.

**Scenario:** Government decides airport profits are too high during economic hardship, reduces maximum TUA rates, or increases concession fees further. OMA has no recourse except litigation (unlikely to succeed against sovereign).

**Probability:** This has already happened once. It will happen again if airports earn sustained excess returns during periods of public discontent with corporate profits.

### 6.2 Concession Non-Renewal (Low Probability, High Impact)

The concession expires in 2048. Upon expiration, all assets revert to the Mexican government "in good operative condition, without any cost to the Federal Government and free from any lien." Renewal is possible for up to 50 additional years but not guaranteed.

**Scenario:** Government decides in 2045 to nationalize airport operations or award concessions to state-owned entities (similar to AIFA). OMA shareholders receive zero compensation for the franchise value.

**Probability:** Low in the near term (23 years remaining). However, VINCI Airports acquiring 29.99% stake in 2022 suggests sophisticated infrastructure investors believe renewal is likely. The Mexican government has generally honored concession frameworks, but political risk is nonzero.

### 6.3 Airline Consolidation Reducing Leverage (Medium Probability)

The proposed VivaAerobus-Volaris merger would create a carrier controlling 70-75% of domestic capacity. A dominant airline could demand more favorable landing fees, extract terminal investments, or threaten route cuts to secondary OMA airports.

**Scenario:** Merged airline renegotiates terms with OMA, compressing aeronautical margins. OMA lacks ability to refuse given traffic concentration.

**Probability:** Merger announced December 2025, pending regulatory approval. Even if blocked, the airline market is structurally concentrated.

### 6.4 Monterrey-Specific Risk (Medium Probability)

42% of revenue from one airport serving one metro area. Monterrey's growth is driven by nearshoring/manufacturing trends that could reverse if:
- US-Mexico trade relations deteriorate (tariffs, border closures)
- Nearshoring trends reverse (automation reduces labor arbitrage)
- Security concerns escalate (cartel activity)
- Natural disaster (Monterrey is earthquake-prone)

**Scenario:** Recession in northern Mexico manufacturing sector reduces business travel; Monterrey passenger traffic declines 20%; OMA earnings fall disproportionately.

**Probability:** Cyclical risk is real. The nearshoring tailwind is currently strong but policy-dependent.

### 6.5 VINCI Control Risk (Low Probability, Uncertain Impact)

VINCI Airports owns 29.99% of OMA as the largest shareholder. While currently aligned with minority shareholders, VINCI's incentives could diverge (related-party transactions, strategic decisions benefiting VINCI's global network at OMA expense, dividend policy changes).

**Scenario:** VINCI attempts creeping acquisition to control, minority squeeze-out, or redirects management attention to non-Mexican opportunities.

**Probability:** Low - VINCI is a sophisticated long-term infrastructure investor with reputation concerns. But governance risk exists in any controlled company.

## 7. Moat & Durability Classification

### Moat Strength: **Moderate**

**Justification:** OMA operates a structural monopoly within its concession territory - no competitor can realistically challenge Monterrey Airport's position serving the third-largest Mexican metro area. However, this moat is a "toll road" type franchise: it exists because the government grants it, and the government can modify or revoke terms. The October 2023 tariff reset demonstrates that pricing power ultimately resides with the regulator, not the operator. True moat businesses can raise prices without external constraint. OMA cannot.

The moat protects against competitive entry but not against regulatory value extraction. This is a critical distinction.

### Earnings Durability: **Medium-High**

**Justification:** Passenger traffic at Mexican airports has grown 4.6-4.7% annually over 16 years, nearly double GDP growth. Air travel in Mexico remains under-penetrated vs. developed markets. Monterrey's nearshoring boom provides a structural tailwind. Even with the 2023 tariff reset, OMA maintained 68%+ EBITDA margins.

Earnings are durable in the sense that:
- Demand is structurally growing
- Cost structure is largely fixed
- Concession runs another 23 years minimum
- Non-aeronautical revenue provides diversification

Earnings are fragile in the sense that:
- Regulatory reset can occur at any time
- 42% exposure to one airport
- 50% exposure to one airline
- 87% domestic traffic (Mexican peso/economic exposure)

## The One Thing That Matters for Durability

**The Mexican government's tolerance for private airport profits determines OMA's long-term returns.**

Everything else - nearshoring, passenger growth, commercial revenue expansion, VINCI's operational expertise - is secondary. OMA operates government-owned assets under a franchise that can be modified at political will. The October 2023 tariff reset was not a one-time event; it was a reminder that the social contract between private operators and the Mexican state can be renegotiated whenever the government perceives excess returns.

If the government permits 65-70% EBITDA margins to persist, OMA compounds value. If the government decides airports should earn utility-like 40-50% margins, OMA's equity value compresses proportionally. No operational excellence or commercial innovation can overcome adverse regulatory treatment.

This is why VINCI's 29.99% stake is informative - a sophisticated global infrastructure investor with deep regulatory relationships apparently believes the franchise will be respected. But minority shareholders should understand they are ultimately dependent on political economy, not competitive economics.

## What Consensus May Be Missing

### The Non-Consensus Insight: OMA's moat is weaker than the other Mexican airport groups, but its growth exposure is better.

**What consensus believes:** All three Mexican airport operators (ASUR, GAP, OMA) have similar moats based on concession exclusivity. OMA trades at a discount because it's smaller, less diversified internationally, and more exposed to domestic traffic.

**What consensus may be missing:**

1. **OMA is the nearshoring play, not a tourism play.** ASUR is Cancun (international tourism). GAP is Guadalajara/Los Cabos (mixed tourism/business). OMA is Monterrey (industrial/manufacturing). The nearshoring wave transforming northern Mexico manufacturing disproportionately benefits OMA. Monterrey industrial space vacancy is 0.82%. The region received 22% of total nearshoring investment 2021-2024. This is structural demand growth that tourism-exposed peers don't capture.

2. **VivaAerobus concentration is a feature, not just a bug.** Yes, 50% exposure to one airline is risky. But VivaAerobus is the fastest-growing Mexican carrier, focused on budget domestic travel - exactly the segment benefiting from Mexican middle-class growth and nearshoring-driven business travel. The exclusive Terminal C relationship creates mutual lock-in. VivaAerobus needs Monterrey; Monterrey needs VivaAerobus. The merger with Volaris, if approved, would create the dominant domestic carrier with Monterrey as a key hub.

3. **Regulatory risk may be LOWER than peers going forward.** The October 2023 tariff reset was painful but also reset expectations. The government has already extracted its pound of flesh. OMA's post-reset margins (68-70%) are closer to "normalized" levels the government appears willing to tolerate. ASUR and GAP, still sporting higher margins, may face their own resets. OMA has already been de-risked on this dimension.

4. **VINCI provides cheap optionality.** Having the world's largest private airport operator as your controlling shareholder provides: operational expertise, global best practices, political relationships, and potential takeout premium if VINCI decides to consolidate. The market may underweight this strategic value.

**Directional implication:** This insight is cautiously bullish for durability. OMA's structural exposure to nearshoring-driven manufacturing growth is under-appreciated. The regulatory reset, while painful, may have cleared the decks for a period of stable, if lower-margin, compounding. The key risk remains political - a new government could change the calculus.
