# Florida Residential Property Insurance: Industry Structure & Cycle Analysis

**Date:** 2026-02-24

## Industry Classification

- **Structural trajectory:** Recovery / Early Growth (post-reform regime change)
- **Competitive structure:** Fragmented, with new entrants accelerating fragmentation
- **Cyclicality:** HIGH -- catastrophe exposure makes this inherently one of the most volatile insurance lines

## What Drives Profitability

Florida residential property insurance profitability is governed by three forces in order of magnitude: (1) catastrophe losses, (2) litigation/loss adjustment costs, and (3) reinsurance pricing. Premium adequacy matters, but in any given year a single hurricane can overwhelm years of underwriting discipline. From 2016 to 2022, Florida domestic carriers produced zero cumulative underwriting profit (per FLOIR data). The industry combined ratio hit 122.0 in 2020 and loss ratios reached 125.5% in 2022 post-Ian.

The 2022-2023 tort reforms (SB 2-A, SB 7052) represent a genuine structural break in litigation economics. Defense and cost-containment expense ratios fell from 8.4% in 2022 to 3.1% in 2023 and 3.4% in 2024. Total litigation filings are down 36% since 2021, with every month of 2025 showing continued year-over-year declines. This is real and durable -- the elimination of one-way attorney fees fundamentally changes the plaintiff bar's economic incentive to pursue marginal claims.

## Decomposing the Combined Ratio Improvement: Structural vs. Cyclical

This is the critical analytical question. The combined ratios being reported (UVE 87.5%, AII 62.8%) reflect the simultaneous convergence of three favorable factors:

**Structural (durable):** Tort reform reduced litigation expense by approximately 5-8 points of combined ratio. This is sticky -- the legal framework has changed. Estimate 5-8 points of permanent improvement, with the caveat that the plaintiff bar adapts over time.

**Cyclical (temporary):** No major Florida hurricane landfall in 2024-2025. This is the dominant factor in the unusually low combined ratios. Florida homeowners loss ratios have historically ranged from 39% (benign year, 2016) to 125% (Ian year, 2022). The current benign cat environment is not sustainable over a multi-year horizon. A "normal" cat year adds 15-25 points to the combined ratio versus current levels.

**One-time tailwind (fading):** Citizens depopulation has delivered high-quality, seasoned policies to private carriers at adequate rates with limited adverse selection risk. This is a finite pool that is now largely depleted.

**My estimate of sustainable mid-cycle combined ratio post-reform: 95-102%, versus the 85-90% being reported currently.** The gap is almost entirely explained by benign cat experience.

## Reinsurance Market Dynamics

Reinsurance costs are declining from post-Ian peaks. Florida carriers secured average 10.7% risk-adjusted reductions at the June 2025 renewal. January 2026 renewals saw further 10-15% declines. Supply is outpacing demand as losses from Hurricanes Milton and Helene tracked in-line with estimates, boosting reinsurer confidence.

This is modestly positive but has a floor -- reinsurers have structurally repriced Florida catastrophe risk post-Ian. Reinsurance will remain a significant expense line (typically 20-30% of gross premiums for Florida specialists), and any large loss event would re-harden rates rapidly.

## Citizens Depopulation: Runway Assessment

Citizens peaked at 1.4 million policies in September 2023 and has fallen to approximately 385,000 by year-end 2025 -- a 73% decline. Nine companies are approved for additional assumptions in early 2026, but the remaining pool is smaller and likely lower-quality (harder to place in private market). The massive tailwind of 2024-2025 depopulation -- over 1 million policies transferred -- is largely behind us. This shifts from strong tailwind to modest tailwind to neutral over 2026-2027. The organic growth story must replace depopulation-driven growth.

## Competitive Dynamics: Fragmentation Accelerating

Seventeen new carriers have entered Florida since reforms passed. Rate filing data shows the 30-day average requested rate increase has collapsed from 21.8% two years ago to 0.8% currently. Seventy-three rate decrease filings and 94 zero-change filings were submitted in 2025. This is the classic competitive response to improved profitability -- new capital enters, pricing discipline erodes. The speed of this competitive deterioration is notable and underappreciated.

## What Happens in a Normal or Bad Cat Year

This is where the current earnings power gets stress-tested:

- **Normal cat year (1-in-5 type event):** Industry combined ratios revert to 100-110%. Reported earnings fall 40-60% from current levels. Reinsurance costs re-accelerate at next renewal.
- **Bad cat year (Ian-type, 1-in-15-20):** Combined ratios spike to 120%+. Multiple smaller carriers face solvency stress. Reinsurance market re-hardens sharply. The cycle resets.

Historical pattern: After Hurricane Andrew (1992), the 2004-2005 hurricane seasons, and Ian (2022), the Florida market followed an identical script -- crisis, reform, new entrants, strong earnings in benign years, competitive erosion of pricing, then the next major storm exposes the fragility. The Poe Insurance Group failure in 2008 ($850M in unpaid claims) is the canonical example of a Florida carrier that looked strong in benign years.

## Current Cycle Position

**Early-to-mid cycle in a post-reform regime.** The tort reforms are a genuine structural improvement, but we are simultaneously at the most favorable point in the catastrophe cycle (two consecutive benign years). Markets are pricing current earnings as if they are more sustainable than they are.

**Confidence level: MODERATE.** The structural reform benefit is high-confidence. The current combined ratio level as representative of normalized earnings is LOW confidence.

## The One Industry Factor That Matters

**Hurricane frequency and severity is the single variable that dominates all others.** Tort reform, reinsurance costs, competitive dynamics -- all of these are second-order relative to whether a major hurricane hits Florida in any given year. A Category 4+ landfall in a populated area would immediately: (a) spike combined ratios above 120%, (b) re-harden reinsurance markets, (c) push several of the 17 new entrants toward insolvency, and (d) reverse the depopulation trend as policies flow back to Citizens. Every other industry dynamic is noise relative to this binary risk.

## What Consensus May Be Missing

**Consensus view:** Tort reform has structurally fixed Florida property insurance. Current earnings reflect a "new normal."

**What consensus gets wrong:** The competitive response is happening faster than expected and will compress margins even without a hurricane. Seventeen new entrants plus rate decrease filings signal that the pricing cycle is already turning. The benign cat years have masked how quickly competitive dynamics are deteriorating. The "structural improvement" narrative is correct on litigation costs but is being used to justify earnings levels that also embed 15-25 points of favorable cat luck. When (not if) a normal cat year arrives, the market will discover that mid-cycle earnings are materially lower than current run-rates, and the new entrants will amplify the downside by competing away pricing power in the interim. This is BEARISH for the sustainability of current earnings, though moderately BULLISH versus pre-reform economics.

## Key Monitoring Variables

- **NOAA/NHC Atlantic hurricane season forecasts and real-time storm tracking** (June-November)
- **FLOIR litigation filing data** -- quarterly; watch for any reversal in the downtrend
- **Citizens policy count** -- monthly; stabilization signals depopulation exhaustion
- **Rate filing data (30-day and 180-day averages)** -- leading indicator of pricing cycle
- **Florida domestic carrier surplus levels** -- FLOIR quarterly; signals capacity adequacy
- **June 1 reinsurance renewal pricing** -- annual benchmark for catastrophe risk transfer costs
