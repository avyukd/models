# Permian Basin Land Royalty and Water Disposal Industry Analysis

## Industry Definition and Scope

This analysis covers the **Permian Basin surface acreage, land royalty, and produced water disposal/handling industry** -- specifically the Delaware Basin sub-region where LandBridge and related companies operate. The industry encompasses:

1. **Surface land ownership** generating revenue from easements, resource extraction (caliche, sand), and land use permits
2. **Produced water royalties** from water handling infrastructure on owned acreage
3. **Sourced water sales** for well completions
4. **Oil and gas royalty interests** on subsurface mineral rights

The unit of analysis is the industry, not any specific company.

---

## What Fundamentally Drives Profitability in This Industry?

### The Primary Economic Mechanism

Profitability in this industry derives from **geographic scarcity of surface rights in areas requiring water infrastructure**. Unlike pure midstream water businesses that can theoretically build anywhere, land royalty economics depend on:

1. **Irreplaceable location** -- owning surface acreage where operators must transit, dispose, or source water
2. **Growing water volumes per barrel of oil** -- as wells mature, water-to-oil ratios (WOR) increase, creating more water handling demand per unit of production
3. **Regulatory constraints on disposal capacity** -- seismic regulations limiting where new injection wells can be permitted
4. **Produced water as an unavoidable byproduct** -- unlike natural gas (which can be flared), produced water must be handled

### The Water-to-Oil Ratio Dynamic

This is the critical secular driver separating water infrastructure from traditional oil services:

- **Delaware Basin WOR averages 4-6 barrels of water per barrel of oil**
- Some Delaware Basin wells reach **10:1 water-to-oil ratios**
- WOR increases over well life as reservoir pressure declines
- Delaware Basin produces **2.8x more water per lateral foot** than Midland Basin

**Key insight**: Even in flat oil production scenarios, produced water volumes continue growing due to rising WOR on existing wells. Per B3 Insight analysis, produced water volumes are likely to increase by approximately 1 MMb/d annually to 2030 even if crude oil production stays flat.

### Revenue Stream Economics

| Revenue Type | Economic Driver | Cyclicality |
|--------------|-----------------|-------------|
| Produced water royalties | Volume x negotiated rate ($/bbl) | Low -- volumes grow even in flat oil price environments |
| Surface easements | Infrastructure buildout pace | Moderate -- tied to capex cycles |
| Sourced water sales | New well completions | High -- directly tied to drilling activity |
| O&G royalties | Production x commodity price | High -- direct commodity exposure |
| Resource sales (caliche) | Road/pad construction activity | Moderate -- tied to development pace |

For a diversified land royalty business, **produced water royalty growth provides the most structurally resilient revenue stream** while sourced water and O&G royalties provide cyclical upside.

## Industry Pricing Power Assessment

### Produced Water Disposal Pricing

Current disposal economics (per B3 Insight and industry data):

- **Disposal fees**: $0.50-$1.00/bbl at the wellhead
- **Pipeline transport**: ~$0.30/bbl
- **Trucking**: up to $2.50/bbl
- **All-in disposal cost to operators**: $0.80-$3.00/bbl depending on location and infrastructure access

### What Creates Pricing Power

1. **Location monopoly on surface rights** -- if you own the only available disposal corridor, you set prices
2. **Seismic permit restrictions** -- new injection wells increasingly difficult to permit in Delaware Basin, protecting existing capacity
3. **Long-term take-or-pay contracts** -- 10-15 year agreements with minimum volume commitments
4. **Integrated pipeline networks** -- operators prefer single-provider solutions over trucking multiple legs

### What Limits Pricing Power

1. **Producer economics at margin** -- at $60 WTI with 5:1 WOR, water disposal becomes a material cost; excessive pricing accelerates well shut-ins
2. **Recycling competition** -- at $0.75-$1.50/bbl, recycled water becomes competitive with disposal for frac operations
3. **Operator self-ownership** -- in northern Delaware, operators own >50% of water infrastructure; potential divestiture competition
4. **Regulatory mandated discharge** -- Texas HB 49 (June 2025) creates liability protections for treated produced water discharge, potentially opening new disposal pathways

## Supply Elasticity and Capacity Dynamics

### Current Capacity Situation

Per RRC and industry data:

- **2024 produced water volumes**: ~20-21 MMb/d in Permian Basin
- **2030 projection**: 26-38 MMb/d (wide range reflects uncertainty)
- **2035 projection**: 32 MMb/d+ with 300 MMbbl/day excess by some estimates
- **Current disposal shortfall signals**: Chevron reduced deep injection by 75% in seismic areas; reservoir pressure rising in shallow zones

### Why New Supply is Constrained

**Regulatory barriers** (effective June 1, 2025 per RRC):
- Area of review expanded from 0.25 miles to 0.5-2.0 mile dual buffer
- New wells within 25km of seismic events require enhanced review
- Deep disposal (below Wolfcamp) suspended in Northern Culberson-Reeves SRA
- Bottomhole pressure monitoring required for new wells

**Geological limits**:
- Shallow reservoirs in some areas approaching capacity ("pressure buildup")
- Deep disposal linked to induced seismicity, increasingly restricted
- 80% of water disposal in earthquake-prone areas shifted from deep to shallow since 2021

**Capital intensity**:
- Pipeline networks require multi-year buildout and significant capital
- Disposal wells require geological assessment, permitting, and drilling
- New entrants face chicken-and-egg problem: need acreage for infrastructure, need infrastructure contracts for financing

### Capacity Response Timeline

Based on industry reports, meaningful new disposal capacity takes:
- **Shallow wells in unrestricted areas**: 12-18 months
- **Pipeline to distant disposal zones**: 24-36 months (announced: up to 5 MMb/d new "long-haul" capacity by 2027)
- **New disposal technologies at scale**: 3-5+ years

**Key finding**: Supply is moderately inelastic in the short-term (1-2 years) due to regulatory complexity, but more responsive at 3-5 year horizons as infrastructure buildout catches up.

32 water M&A transactions since 2020 have accelerated consolidation. Specialized midstream companies have grown from 23% of disposal market share (Q2 2016) to >40% (2020). Operators increasingly divesting water infrastructure to focus on core E&P.

### Competitive Positioning Factors

Winners in this industry typically have:
1. **Large contiguous acreage positions** in high-activity corridors
2. **Regulatory grandfather status** on existing disposal wells
3. **Long-term contracts with investment-grade operators**
4. **Low leverage to fund infrastructure buildout**
5. **Proximity to non-restricted disposal zones** (Central Basin, distance-haul opportunities)

## Cyclicality Assessment

### Cyclical vs. Structural Drivers

| Factor | Character | Explanation |
|--------|-----------|-------------|
| New well completions | **Cyclical** | Directly tied to oil price, rig count |
| Sourced water demand | **Cyclical** | Follows completions activity |
| Produced water volumes | **Structural** | Grows even with flat production (rising WOR) |
| Disposal capacity scarcity | **Structural** | Regulatory-driven, worsening over time |
| O&G royalty revenues | **Cyclical** | Direct commodity price exposure |
| Surface easement buildout | **Moderate** | Tied to infrastructure investment cycles |

### Historical Cyclicality Evidence

During 2020 oil price collapse:
- Drilling activity dropped 60-70% in Permian
- **Produced water volumes declined only ~15-20%** -- existing wells keep producing water
- Water disposal infrastructure remained essential (unlike drilling services)
- Operators with long-term contracts maintained cashflow

### Cyclicality Conclusion

**This is a LOW-to-MODERATE cyclicality industry with significant structural tailwinds.**

Unlike pure drilling services (high cyclicality) or pipelines with throughput risk (moderate cyclicality), the land royalty/water disposal model benefits from:
1. Produced water volumes that grow independent of oil prices
2. Regulatory constraints that limit competitive supply response
3. Fee-based revenue with volume floors on long-term contracts

The structural component dominates: **even in a scenario where Permian oil production plateaus (as most forecasters expect by 2027-2030), produced water volumes continue growing due to rising WOR.**

**Confidence level: MODERATE-HIGH**. The structural trends are clear; uncertainty exists around exact timing of capacity constraints and regulatory impacts.

### Mid-Cycle Qualitative Definition

In a "normal" mid-cycle environment:
- Produced water volumes grow in line with production (2-3% annually)
- Sufficient disposal capacity exists with modest pricing power
- New permits available in most areas with standard review
- Operators balance self-handling vs. third-party solutions
- M&A activity moderate; reasonable valuations

### Current vs. Mid-Cycle

**We are currently ABOVE mid-cycle conditions** for water infrastructure economics due to:
1. Regulatory restrictions creating artificial supply constraints
2. WOR increasing faster than historical averages (mature Delaware development)
3. Disposal capacity lagging volume growth

This creates **favorable industry conditions** but also raises the question: **is this the new normal or a temporary premium?**

## Structural vs. Cyclical: The Core Question

### Structural Tailwinds (Independent of Oil Prices)

1. **WOR growth is secular** -- as Delaware Basin wells mature, WOR continues rising regardless of new drilling activity
2. **Regulatory restrictions are ratcheting tighter** -- unlikely to reverse given seismicity concerns
3. **Infrastructure deficit will take years to close** -- 1.9 MMb/d+ shortfall projected even with aggressive buildout
4. **Permian's dominance continues** -- even in plateau scenario, basin produces ~40% of U.S. oil
5. **Data center demand (potential)** -- Waha natural gas oversupply creates opportunity for in-basin power generation and industrial development

### Cyclical Headwinds (Oil Price Dependent)

1. **New drilling activity** -- sourced water and easement revenues follow rig count
2. **Operator capital discipline** -- affects pace of infrastructure buildout
3. **Commodity price impact on O&G royalties** -- direct exposure for royalty owners
4. **Producer economics at margin** -- at very low oil prices ($40-50 WTI), some wells become uneconomic even with low water disposal costs

### The Verdict

**Structural tailwinds dominate for produced water volumes and disposal capacity scarcity.**

**Cyclical exposure remains for sourced water, easements, and O&G royalties.**

A well-positioned land royalty business should have:
- 50-60% of revenue from structural sources (produced water royalties, long-term disposal contracts)
- 40-50% of revenue from cyclical sources (sourced water, easements, O&G royalties) providing upside in good years

## The One Industry Factor That Matters

### The Single Most Important Variable

**Disposal capacity vs. produced water volume growth in the Delaware Basin Stateline region.**

Everything else is secondary noise. The industry's economics hinge on whether:
1. **Volume growth (5-8% annually) exceeds capacity additions (constrained by regulation)**
2. **Regulatory restrictions continue tightening or stabilize**
3. **Alternative disposal pathways (treated discharge, long-haul pipelines) scale successfully**

### What Would Cause Inflection

**Bullish inflection**:
- Continued seismic activity leading to further deep disposal restrictions
- Permian oil production growth exceeding expectations (upward revision in WOR projections)
- Regulatory barriers to new permit applications increasing

**Bearish inflection**:
- Breakthrough in recycling/treatment economics making surface discharge at scale viable
- Relaxation of seismic restrictions as monitoring improves
- Major new disposal capacity coming online (5+ MMb/d) faster than expected

### How to Monitor

1. **RRC permit activity in seismic response areas** -- tracking new permits vs. suspensions
2. **Texas TexNet seismic event frequency** -- M3.5+ events trigger regulatory response
3. **B3 Insight water volume and capacity data** -- industry-standard tracking
4. **Major operator water infrastructure divestiture announcements** -- signals capacity coming to market
5. **Long-haul disposal pipeline project progress** (Central Basin routing)

## What Consensus May Be Missing

### The Non-Consensus Insight

**Consensus view**: Permian water disposal is a growth industry tied to oil production growth.

**What consensus may be missing**: **The industry is entering a period of structural scarcity that is largely independent of oil production levels.** Even if Permian oil production plateaus (as expected by 2027-2030), produced water volumes will continue growing due to:

1. Rising WOR on existing wells (aging development)
2. Continued development of remaining inventory (even at flat production rates)
3. Seismic restrictions constraining new disposal capacity
4. Shallow reservoir pressure buildup limiting capacity in permitted areas

**This means**: The industry's best years may be ahead even if oil production stops growing. The consensus mental model of "water follows oil" is directionally correct but misses the WOR secular trend.

### Implication

This is **bullish for industry participants** with:
- Large acreage positions in capacity-constrained areas
- Long-term contracts that capture rising volumes at fixed fees
- Permitted disposal capacity in non-seismic areas
- Optionality on alternative disposal pathways

- Data center development on large contiguous surface acreage
- Premium pricing for firm gas commitments to generation assets

### Industry Impact Assessment

**This is a long-duration optionality, not a near-term driver.** Relevant for land royalty owners because:
1. Large contiguous acreage required for data center development
2. Power, water, and fiber infrastructure needs align with existing surface use revenue models
3. 5-10 year development timeline makes this a 2030+ story

**Caution**: Do not model data center revenue as base case; treat as free optionality that may or may not materialize.

## Historical Industry Margin and Profitability

### Industry Profitability Ranges

| Business Model | EBITDA Margin Range | Comment |
|----------------|---------------------|---------|
| Pure-play water midstream | 40-55% | Capital-intensive; infrastructure depreciation |
| Land royalty / surface rights | 70-85%+ | Low-capex; fee-based; minimal operating costs |
| Integrated (midstream + royalty) | 50-65% | Blend depending on mix |

### What Drives Margin Variability

1. **Contract structure** -- take-or-pay vs. volume-based
2. **Infrastructure ownership vs. royalty** -- owning infrastructure = more capital, lower margins on invested capital
3. **Geographic concentration** -- concentrated positions in high-activity areas = better economics
4. **Regulatory exposure** -- wells in seismic areas face potential shutdown risk

### Mean Reversion Mechanisms

Margins compress when:
- New disposal capacity comes online (competitive pressure)
- Operators develop self-handling capabilities
- Treatment/discharge technology improves economics
- Regulatory environment stabilizes

Margins expand when:
- Volume growth outpaces capacity
- Seismic restrictions constrain new permits
- Consolidation reduces competition
- Long-term contracts roll at higher rates

### Final Assessment

If I knew nothing about any company in this industry, industry economics alone would make me **comfortable to favorable** about capital being deployed here. The structural dynamics of rising WOR, regulatory constraints on new capacity, and long-term contract structures create a favorable industry environment that is partially independent of oil price cycles.

The key question is duration: how long does the structural scarcity persist before capacity catches up or technology disrupts disposal economics? Current evidence suggests **at least 5-7 years of favorable conditions** given permitting timelines and the magnitude of the capacity deficit.

This is a rare case where an "oil services adjacent" industry has meaningful structural tailwinds independent of commodity cycles.
