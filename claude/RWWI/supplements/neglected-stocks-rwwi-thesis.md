Rand Worldwide ($RWWI): The Autodesk Middleman, Reinvented
Let’s talk about a weird little software reseller with a weird little financial story. Based on the recent look at the revenue, it may look like a boring reseller business with shrinking revenue. But it’s actually undergoing a working capital overhaul and becoming a higher-margin, lower-risk cash machine. The market may be misunderstanding this overhaul and hasn’t figured it out yet. That company is Rand Worldwide ($RWWI).

Let’s dig.

1. Company Overview
Rand Worldwide is basically Autodesk’s middleman. It buys licenses from Autodesk and resells them to end customers, while also providing consulting, training, and integration services. It helps customers decide which software with which customization to purchase.

It’s organized into five divisions, but IMAGINiT (the Autodesk channel partner division) is the core engine.

2. Durability: Autodesk Isn’t Going Anywhere
Autodesk has a 10,000-foot-wide moat in AEC (architecture, engineering, construction). They're like Microsoft for design workflows — AutoCAD, Revit, Civil 3D, Fusion, etc. It is not a monopoly, but it is the biggest player and is considered an essential part of many businesses.

Rand is one of their largest North American partners and has been riding shotgun for 20+ years. That Autodesk relationship is sticky as hell, and even though the billing has shifted under the new agency model, the partnership is still locked in.

One might say, “Scav, if Rand is so profitable, why wouldn’t Autodesk just start doing what Rand is doing and kill Rand’s business?” For Autodesk, farming out distribution and customer service to partners like Rand allows it to focus on what it does best. I would compare this situation to the recent Nike and Foot Locker (and many other footwear distributors/retailers that Nike tried to cut ties with) relationship saga. Nike thought they could make more profit by selling directly to the customers. What they underestimated was that they were as dependent on Foot Locker as much as Foot Locker depends on them. What Nike does well is developing new products and marketing. Turns out, they were not good at becoming a retailer.

Rand allows the customers to identify which Autodesk products and customizations they need, provides solutions to various issues that may arise, and markets the product for Autodesk. Autodesk needs someone to hold thousands of customers’ hands, and Rand does that part well.

3. Moat
Rand’s moat isn’t in proprietary tech. It's in distribution, relationships, and services. Thousands of SMB and enterprise customers already rely on Rand for software, support, and training. Customers rarely switch service providers, especially in CAD/BIM, where institutional workflows are entrenched. The network effect of long-term service relationships and customer stickiness is very real here. The moat comes from 1) Autodesk’s moat (network effect, switching cost, industry essential, small cost compared to the project cost, etc), 2) Rand’s long-term symbiotic relationship with Autodesk, and 3) Rand’s long-term relationship with the customers with low churn.

You simply can’t start up a new “Autodesk reseller + services” shop overnight and eat Rand’s lunch.

4. Quality: The Balance Sheet Overhaul
I am a big fan of companies with an improving working capital and balance sheet situation. For example, when there is a struggling company hires a new management team that says “we are going to grow our way out of this trouble to turn the ship around”, I walk away (not that Rand is a turn-around). However, if they say “we will first clean up the working capital and balance sheet mess to make it more favorable to do business, then we will try to grow only after that”, my ears perk up.

So with that, this is where things get interesting. This is where the transformation is happening. And it’s not obvious unless you read the filings.

Prior to March 2023:

From 2018 to March 2023, Autodesk was pushing tons of 3-year Autodesk subscription deals with a lower price and 0% financing. With this 3-year subscription sale, Rand recognized all that revenue upfront, invoiced the full 3-year license price upfront to the customer, collected cash upfront, and paid Autodesk in full or in installments. As Rand recorded earnings and collected cash upfront for a 3-year term business, it likely pulled future earnings and cash flow into the present.

Some people I talked to were very excited about the earnings growth during this period, but I was a bit wary at this time. Becasue I wasn’t a big fan of this form of financial engineering. Whether it was voluntary by Rand or forced by Autodesk because Autodesk didn’t allow Rand to pay for the COGS to Autodesk annually, I am not sure. Regardless, I was a bit wary of it after I read this excerpt from their 2020 Annual Report.




(Source: 2020 Annual Report)

I interpreted this as the revenue increased by $84m, not simply because there were more sales, but entirely because they recognized the revenues upfront. If the company continually signs new 3-year contracts each year, the numbers would look great perpetually. But when they either don’t have new customers signing up for the 3-year term or the customers don’t renew, the rolling upfront earnings will fall significantly. So I just watched to see how earnings would pan out. It was painful because the stock kept going up to ~$25 in 2023.

March 2023 - June 2024:

Rand introduced annual installment billing arrangements for those who purchased multi-year terms.


After this, there were three ways to record the sales

One-year deals / billed upfront

Multi-year deals / billed upfront

Multi-year deals / billed in annual installments (new)

This means Rand will offer customers the ability to pay in installments only if their vendor (Autodesk) allows Rand to also pay in installments for the software

I am not 100% sure who pushed for this. But based on the language in the filings, it seems like Autodesk changed first, and Rand followed suit when it became financially feasible for them.

“We began offering annual installment billing arrangements ... when our vendor provided matching installment arrangements on the costs of fulfillment.”

Again, I wasn’t a big fan of option #2. But after a new language showed up on the 2023 Annual Report (below), option #2 appeared to have an advantage: it would generate a float. In one way, this was smart because Rand is charging the customers upfront for 3-years of service, while paying Autodesk over 3 years; thus, creating a float.


(Source 2023 Annual Report)

Still, options #1 and #3 would have been my preferred way. Not pulling any earnings and cash flows from the future to have the current earnings look higher, for the service that you provide in the future.

On top of that, providing option #3 as a payment option brought on pretty big implications in the balance sheet.







(Source: 2023 Annual Report)







(Source: 2024 Annual Report)

Contract assets: from $11.5M in June 2023 → $145.3M in June 2024

Contract-related liabilities: $13.1M → $144.5M over the same period

The balance sheet got distorted: contract assets and liabilities surged, masking the company’s true asset-light nature.

Why? 1) Many customers still opted for a 3-year contract. From the year end of June 2024, the company’s revenue from 3-year deals was $59.5m higher. 2) Many customers likely opted for the option # 3 payment, where they can pay Rand annually rather than upfront. If I were a customer of Rand with common sense, I would’ve opted for option #3 as well. Why pass on the chance of improving your working capital cycle by paying annually instead of paying upfront?

Because of these combinations, Rand was building up huge contract assets (lots of unbilled revenues for annual installment billing arrangements for non-cancellable multi-year license sales) and contract-related liabilities (COGS that are payable to Autodesk on an annual installment basis for multi-year license resale). This put a ton of current and non-current assets and liabilities on the balance sheet and made this business look like an asset and liability-heavy business, while the reality is that this is a very asset-light business. Also, while Rand would incur non-license related costs at the time of sale (non-license costs like SG&A), the cash would come over three years.

At this point, even though I was following the company closely, it was still in a “Too-Hard” pile where I wasn’t sure how I could accurately analyze and predict how this change would impact the business.

June 10, 2024 – Present: Full Transition to the Agency Model

What I am about to discuss now is why Rand is stimulating my interest now. The above issues that I mentioned? That’s going to get resolved (not by Rand’s choice, but forced by Autodesk). Autodesk stopped the 3-year deal with 0% financing in March 2023. We know that by now. What is key is that Autodesk flipped to the agency model in June 2024. Read the following excerpt.


(Source: 2024 Annual Report)

This has several implications for the company’s structure and how it produces its earnings and maintains its balance sheet. Rand no longer handles billing, fulfillment, or collections. Rand just earns a commission on each sale. They just pass the information needed for the sale (i.e., customer name, the customization needed, price points, etc) to Autodesk and get paid a commission. No billing, no collections, no fulfillment headaches and risks. Clean and cash-efficient.

Three big implications:

1) The apparent paper revenue will decrease significantly (you can see that in the recent quarterly reports). With the changes to the agency/commission model, there is no product sale and related COGS. These will become commission revenue with no COGS. Revenue will decline, but the gross profit margin will increase, and thus the same gross profit amount will be achieved. Revenue looks lower now, but it’s cleaner. Just cash in, cash out.

I am not certain how this affects the three revenue recognition options we previously discussed. Looking at Autodesk’s 2024 10-K, though, it seems like they have both annual installments and upfront options.


2) No billing, no collections, no fulfillment = No longer will have large balances of contract assets (lots of unbilled revenues for annual installment billing arrangement for non-cancellable multi-year license sales) and contract-related liabilities. Also, this means Rand could incur less SG&A from fewer people managing the billing, collections, and fulfillment.

3) Messy balance sheet and working capital overhaul = Cashflow from Op will be messy in the near future. It will have a few periods with messy working capital changes with winding down the old working capital structure as it phases out the old model and fully transitions to the agency model. This will cause a bumpy cash flow from operations with some period of negative CFO. It likely will affect its ability to pay out to shareholders in the near future. But after the window of this working capital overhaul, Rand will have a much cleaner balance sheet. Less worry and effort in managing the working capital.

—-----------------------------------------------

A little deeper discussion of 3), if I may. Feel free to skip this section if you would like. Not as important. So what happens when you unwind all that old working capital? You get weird timing effects on the cash flow statement.

Case in point: Q1 FY2025

Net income was $ 5.8M. But cash flow from operations? Negative $1.3M. Why?

They paid down a ton of old payables and accruals. Accounts payable and accrued expenses dropped $ 17.4M. That’s real cash out.

They also paid down contract-related liabilities by $8.6M, which is the legacy amounts owed to Autodesk for old 3-year deals.

So yes, cash flow was negative. But that’s not a red flag. That’s a positive structural unwind. They’re clearing out the sludge from the old model.

One might ask: “how about the cash flow in Q2 FY2025? Why were both ‘contract assets’ and ‘contract-related liabilities’ negative in the cash flow statement?”

Here’s what (I think) is going on:

Contract assets (-$11.5M): Rand booked more revenue but didn’t collect the cash yet. Under the agency model, Rand no longer recognizes gross product revenue, so it doesn’t book big contract assets for 3-year software sales anymore. But this could reflect earned but uncollected commission from Autodesk or other services performed in the current year, but not billed until the subsequent year.

Contract-related liabilities (-$4.7M): They continued paying off what they still owed to Autodesk from legacy deals. But with no new liabilities being added under the agency model, the overall contract-related liability balance keeps falling. You can observe this in the recent quarterly filings. In June 2024, the total contract-related liabilities were ~ $144M vs $119M in March 2025.

So you’re seeing a bridge period where old obligations are being paid off, and new commissions are growing but not yet fully cash-settled. This mismatch is temporary and can cause negative cash flows, but I believe this is healthy in the long run. As the agency model fully works its way through the pipes, we will have a much cleaner balance sheet and the cash flow from op should return to normal.

Bottom line: this balance sheet unwind is a good thing, in my unqualified opinion. It’s moving the business toward clean, cash-generative operations with fewer contract-related assets and liabilities, with improvement in the earnings quality and the free cash flow timing.

5. Capital Allocation
So here’s the good and the sketchy.

Rand is crazy shareholder-friendly. It is a Peter Kamin company. If anyone knows Kamin’s playbook from other companies he took control of (e.i, Callaway Nursery, Tile Shop Holdings), this is not a surprise. It pays out most of its free cash flow as dividends.

(Other stuff he did: liquidated Ambassadors Group. MAM Software got acquired)

No dumb big acquisitions. Decent quality bolt-on acquisitions. No equity dilution. Just cold, hard cash to shareholders in a capital-light business. I would love opportunistic buybacks instead of dividends, but it is highly illiquid, which makes buybacks hard (tender offers, maybe?).

6. Growth
I don’t really have much to comment on this, as I am not sure. It currently sits at around 10 x EV/FCF, giving a 10% yield. Paying out at about a 6-8% dividend yield. 2-4% getting retained for growth. I don’t expect hypergrowth here. But even without growth, it gives off a decent yield the growth will be self-funded.

Here is an obligatory comment: I don’t exactly know how the recent Trump tariff will affect Rand. Will companies stop purchasing/renewing Autodesk licenses? Unlikely. Less consulting and service? Perhaps. I am just not sure. I won’t make any predictions on stuff that I am not sure about.

But here’s what I can expect:

1) Commission-based Autodesk revenue should grow with Autodesk’s pricing and customer base.

2) Services revenue (training, consulting, support) should steadily grind upward with increasing customer base and inflation.

3) Bolt-on acquisitions

Over time, as legacy liabilities roll off and the agency model settles in, free cash flow will become more consistent and visible.

Low capex. High margins. Improving working capital. Recurring services. It’s a grow-slow-but-strong kind of setup.

7. Valuation
Reported revenue is shrinking because of the agency model shift. But earnings aren’t. Operating income in Q3 FY2025 hit $18.6M, with net income of $13.6M.

Its peak FCF was ~$50M.

While the FCF has been bumpy recently, FCF should stabilize as the new revenue recognition options and the agency models settle. I probably will value this based on its FCF as the company pays out most of its FCF. Currently, as of 5/12/2025, its EV is ~ $500m.

This gives us ~ 10 x EV/FCF. That is a 10% yield without any growth. Let’s say it grows at 2-3% organically and inorganically + 2-3% inflation. This could net out 12-16% return. That is too cheap.

Let’s say I didn’t understand the change brought on by the upfront vs annual payment model, changes to the agency model, or we are about to have a major recession and the company’s FCF will get hit by 40%. This will give us ~16.7 x EV/FCF with 6% yield. With 0% growth + 2-3% inflation, it could net out 8-9% return.

If it can decrease the SG&A, since with the new agency model (no billing, fulfillment, collection), it could possibly return higher.

8. Risks
Some real ones to think about:

1 - Autodesk dependency: if Autodesk falls out of favor, Rand will struggle. Also, if they change commission terms or cut Rand out, it's game over. Autodesk has assured Rand that the overall reimbursement amount with the new agency model will not have any material impact on the amount of the overall compensation.

2 - Lumpy transition with aggressive dividend: the timing of legacy contract payments and commission cash can cause quarterly noise. At least while the working capital is winding down, if they don’t dial the dividend back, they could stress their cash position. They drew $3.6M from the credit line while paying out dividends (and paying down their note), during a period when working capital was still winding down and is yet to normalize. That’s a bit aggressive. You don’t want to see a company leveraging up to fund distributions in a transition year. Now it is still at a reasonable scale. I’m not saying they’re being reckless, but it’s worth watching.

No moat outside of Autodesk: their whole position is built around a single ecosystem.

3 - Lastly, this is a Peter Kamin company. The biggest risk is the stock going dark. This happened to Callaway Nursery. They went dark recently. Psychemedics got delisted and likely will go dark soon. Rand is a $500m company, so it will be harder for a management takeover (or under). In my opinion, given the high-quality nature of the business, it would be far more profitable for major shareholders (which includes Peter Kamin) to uplist this rather than going dark. This is a speculation, but a reasonable one.

(4 - My analysis could be entirely wrong. I didn’t run this analysis by anyone, so if you think I’ve completely missed the mark or gotten something flat-out wrong, I genuinely welcome the feedback. It wouldn’t be the first time I’ve been wrong, and hopefully, it will be another opportunity to learn something new.

9. Why This Opportunity Exists
Optics and lack of disclosures.

The agency model optically killed reported revenue. They previously recorded $330M+ a year in gross Autodesk sales. Now they just book commission, which optically looks like revenue is falling off a cliff while the real economics is improving. Unwinding of the contract-related liability and asset is temporarily putting strains on the free cash flow, and the company has cut its dividend. Anyone just screening for revenue growth or GAAP cash flow would think this thing is collapsing.

But it’s not. It’s transitioning.

Lastly, this company doesn’t show up in a screener. Doesn’t fill with SEC. No conference calls, no analyst coverage, etc. You have to physically click and read the filings to look at their numbers. At least based on a few screeners I looked at, the latest financials that show up are for 2019.

Conclusion:
$RWWI is a software reseller and services business emerging from a messy accounting shift with a cleaner balance sheet, better margins, and a long-term anchor relationship in Autodesk. The market is chasing shiny AI companies while this thing is quietly spitting cash, cleaning its books, and paying out its cash to shareholders. I prefer to have a few concentrated holdings. But $RWWI doesn’t come without risks. Thus, it is a small part of my portfolio.

I’m in. See you in 5 years.
