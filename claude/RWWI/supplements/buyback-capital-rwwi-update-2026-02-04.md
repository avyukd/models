In mid-September, I wrote about the changes unfolding with respect to Autodesk’s value-added reseller (VAR) channel. Autodesk (ADSK), in a move to seize back margin and business from its outsourced distribution channel, took back control of transactions from its resellers and changed the billing intervals on which its software products could be resold in 2023. This change has been disruptive for many resellers, but I continue to believe it is a boon for the largest and most sophisticated VARs, among whom I would place Rand first in North America.

RWWI’s listing on the OTC boards makes detailed disclosures hard to come by. We can, however, piece together information from some of its listed European peers and indeed from Autodesk itself. Developments so far (it’s early days, I’ll freely admit) have been quite promising.

Autodesk
Any discussion of RWWI naturally has to be contextualised against developments at ADSK, to whose growth RWWI is unavoidably tied. Amid the general bloodbath taking place in commercial software at the business level, ADSK has stood out as an exceptional example of a dominant provider growing both revenue and margins at a time when the “vibe-coding” revolution is posing existential questions for the software industry more broadly. I tend to think ADSK is continuing to gain share in several verticals not solely because its software is superior. Installed-base effects matter. Training moats matter. Industry efficiencies from having a sole supplier matter. As ADSK management continues to layer AI applications into its products, its R&D moat may deepen as well.

We are now in the early innings of the market realising that software/SaaS’s days of 80%+ gross margins as an industry standard are likely over. AI might be as big, if not bigger, than the advent of the internet, but it doesn’t get replicated at zero marginal cost. Far from it. We may derive productivity gains from new AI models, but every application requires an enormous amount of compute—something increasingly in short supply (Google recently restricted access to Gemini 3). The benefits of AI come at a cost. Competitively, if you don’t offer them, someone else will. Many CFOs are now highlighting that the impact on gross margins will be negative, and ADSK is no exception. This morning, CFO Janesh Moorjani acknowledged as much. While absolute gross-profit dollars are expected to rise regardless, the ability for dominant software providers to exhibit operational leverage will come down to their ability to grow gross profits faster than operating costs. Harder than what has historically been the case, but not impossible.

In the early 2010s, Google experienced declining gross margins as it built out massive infrastructure, which later rebounded. I’m not sure the comparison is entirely apt for enterprise SaaS, but perhaps a few truly dominant providers will be able to capitalise on their positions, bridge the gap, and emerge more valuable on the other side. We’ll have to see.

What is clear is that ADSK plans to much more aggressively monetise access to its APIs. The position of the VARs is interesting in this regard. VARs, for the most part, won’t need to build any infrastructure to support sales here. They are, ironically, becoming more capital- and operationally-light—albeit from a high base.

There is always the question of whether AI might reduce the need for design and construction professionals to use software like ADSK at all. This seems far-fetched for now. ADSK’s stated strategy is to increase the number of projects a professional can reasonably complete in a given period—making people more effective, not redundant. In any event, ADSK is likely the best-placed provider to offer applications capable of completing projects with minimal human involvement. Many of its competitive advantages would persist under either outcome.

Meanwhile, Autodesk’s transition to the transaction model is progressing rapidly. This momentum has been bolstered by its shift to annual billings. Beginning in 2018, ADSK offered incentives for distribution partners to sell three-year subscriptions upfront. This pulled revenue forward but also suppressed some of ADSK’s long-term economics. Billings ranged from upfront, multi-year agreements paid annually, to annual terms. Because the reseller channel handled both payments and collections, ADSK had limited control over pricing. ADSK has now flipped both dynamics: it transacts directly with customers and enforces a broadly annual billing cycle, allowing it to price subscriptions internally.

This was a calamity for what ADSK describes as its bronze and silver resellers. It would be generous to label many of these outfits as “value added.” They were essentially marketing shops offering limited training and software support. When ADSK seized payments processing, it killed the one major thing keeping many of these players in business: pricing power. Because resellers set end-customer pricing, less sophisticated players could undercut competitors to attract customers. ADSK has now largely automated renewals as well—previously an easy source of recurring revenue for resellers. Commissions on contract renewals have been adjusted down accordingly.

ADSK CEO Andrew Anagnost made a few interesting comments today in the earnings call that caught my attention:

“Obviously, partners always have lots of questions when we change their incentive structure. Generally speaking, they get what we’re trying to do, and they understand what’s going on here. We’re not trying to take money out of the channel ecosystem. We’re trying to shift how it gets paid out. Makes total sense to them. We haven’t seen any initial kind of changes in their behavior right now at all.”

With some sleuthing—and perhaps some conjecture—I would assume that the reduction in renewal revenues is being offset by much more aggressive incentives for new sales. How does one generate new sales now that pricing is off the table? The obvious answers are: a large and well-trained sales force (not a moat), implementation talent (also not a moat), and complementary proprietary software. RWWI enjoys all three.

In any case, ADSK isn’t doing much minute sales grunt work itself. The walls are caving in on the non-Platinum-tier resellers, and the result is significant consolidation among the scaled players. Many smaller bronze and silver-tier resellers are simply shutting down. Any small player with scale is selling to the platinum tiers. This quarter, Swedish ADSK VAR Addnode (the ADSK VAR business is roughly one-third of its operations) acquired another Autodesk reseller, SolidCAD. This is its second acquisition in North America this year, following an unnamed purchase in the southern United States in July. It is unclear whether Rand’s Imaginit Technologies is following suit; no M&A has been announced since before the pandemic. As small players disappear, more business flows to the better Platinum-tier VARs—and the remaining large players’ bargaining position with ADSK improves.

ADSK posted 16% YoY revenue growth through the transaction model. Overall growth was higher, though this includes one-time benefits from the model change. Growth was particularly strong in certain EM geographies, but AutoCAD and related business lines (especially in construction) continue to perform extremely well.

Rand

Rand continues to work down its contract-related liabilities, which will periodically distort its cash-flow statement. By the end of calendar year ’26 we can expect the balance of these three-year deals to be fully run off, with some lingering effects continuing into 2027 given the mid-year timing of the agency-model transition.
