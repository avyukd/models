# Industry Structure & Cycle Analysis: Michigan Community Banking

## Industry Classification

**Structural Trajectory:** Mature / Consolidating
**Competitive Structure:** Fragmented but rapidly consolidating
**Cyclicality:** Moderate-to-High (tied to rate cycle, credit cycle, and regional economy)

---

## Executive Summary

The Michigan community banking sector sits at an inflection point where multiple cycles are intersecting in non-obvious ways. The consensus view is "rate cuts compress NIM, credit stress is coming, but M&A provides a floor." This analysis finds the consensus is partially wrong on timing and magnitude.

**Key non-consensus insight:** The community bank NIM trough may already be behind us, not ahead. Deposit betas on the way down are proving asymmetric (slower) compared to the way up, creating a more favorable NIM trajectory than feared. Meanwhile, the credit cycle in Michigan specifically may be less severe than national averages suggest due to the state's underweight exposure to the highest-stress CRE segments.

---

## Rate Cycle Position: NIMs Are Stabilizing, Not Collapsing

### Current Rate Environment

The Fed funds rate stands at 3.5%-3.75% after 175bps of cuts since September 2024. Market expectations show only 1-2 additional cuts through 2026, with the December 2025 dot plot projecting just one 25bp cut in 2026.

### Community Bank NIM Dynamics

Community bank NIM reached 3.73% in Q3 2025, up 10bps sequentially and above the pre-pandemic average of 3.63%. This is counter to the bearish narrative of imminent NIM collapse.

**Why NIMs are holding better than expected:**

1. **Asymmetric deposit betas:** Deposit betas proved higher on the way up (cumulative beta continued rising even after rate hikes stopped) but are proving lower on the way down. Banks report targeting deposit betas around 50% for rate cuts versus 60%+ realized on the way up.

2. **Fixed-rate asset repricing tailwind:** Loans originated in 2020-2021 at rock-bottom rates are repricing into a higher rate environment. This provides NII support even as new loan yields decline.

3. **Deposit mix shift stabilizing:** The aggressive shift from non-interest-bearing to CDs that pressured 2023-2024 NIMs has largely played out. CD maturity walls in 2025-2026 create repricing opportunities at lower rates.

### Cycle Position Assessment

**Current Position: Early-to-Mid Cycle for NIM recovery, NOT peak or trough**

The consensus view treats "Fed cutting" as automatically bearish for NIM. This misses the mechanism: what matters is whether asset yields or funding costs move faster. For asset-sensitive community banks with significant fixed-rate loan books repricing, the current environment is actually favorable.

**Key risk:** If the Fed cuts more aggressively than currently priced (3+ cuts in 2026), NIM compression accelerates. But this scenario also implies economic weakness that would hurt credit quality more than NIM.

## Credit Cycle Position: Later Than You Think, But Michigan Has Offsets

### National Credit Picture

Credit metrics are normalizing from unsustainably low levels, not deteriorating into crisis:

- Credit card charge-offs at 4.04% (Q2 2025), declining for three consecutive quarters but elevated vs. prior decade
- Consumer loan delinquencies rising but concentrated in younger/lower-income borrowers
- CRE stress elevated but concentrated in office (11.3% CMBS delinquency) and certain multifamily markets

### CRE: The Real Risk

Community banks have 3-4x the CRE concentration of large banks. Key dynamics:

- **Office:** Structural decline, 20% national vacancy, values down 30% from peak. This is secular, not cyclical.
- **Multifamily:** CMBS delinquency hit 7.12% before declining to 6.64%. Community bank multifamily delinquency at 0.97% with $6.1B in delinquent loans.
- **Retail/Industrial:** Relatively healthy, benefiting from supply chain reshoring and omnichannel retail adaptation.

**"Extend and pretend" remains widespread.** Banks are rolling troubled CRE loans rather than recognizing losses. This delays but doesn't eliminate credit events. The question is whether 2026-2027 brings a wave of forced recognitions.

### Michigan-Specific Credit Dynamics

Michigan's credit outlook is better than national averages for several reasons:

1. **Lower office exposure:** Michigan metros have lower office vacancy than coastal tech hubs. Detroit and Grand Rapids office markets never saw the speculative building of Austin, Phoenix, or SF.

2. **Multifamily not overbuilt:** Michigan avoided the 2021-2022 multifamily construction boom that created oversupply in Sunbelt markets.

3. **Manufacturing/industrial strength:** Michigan's industrial CRE benefits from EV supply chain investment and nearshoring trends. This is the least stressed CRE category.

4. **Housing affordability:** Michigan home prices remain below national averages, creating less household stress and lower mortgage delinquency pressure.

### Cycle Position Assessment

**Current Position: Mid-to-Late Cycle for credit, with potential for extension**

The credit cycle is mature but not yet turning decisively negative. Michigan-specific factors provide some insulation. The biggest risk is a sudden economic shock (recession, auto industry disruption) that forces recognition of "extend and pretend" losses simultaneously.

## Michigan Regional Economy: Structural Headwinds, Cyclical Stability

### Current Conditions

- Unemployment: 5.0% (November 2025), 0.4pp above national average
- Forecast: 5.3-5.4% through 2026-2027
- Job growth: Essentially flat, with 2,000 net job losses forecast for 2026

### The Aging Workforce Constraint

Michigan's fundamental economic challenge is demographic, not cyclical. The state's aging population is creating a structural labor shortage that prevents job growth even when national employment expands. This is a secular headwind that will persist regardless of the business cycle.

### Auto Industry: The Wildcard

Michigan's 1.2 million auto-related jobs represent massive single-industry concentration. Key dynamics:

- Detroit-3 sales declining: 5.6M units (2025) falling to 5.4M (2028)
- EV transition uncertainty: Ford took $19B impairment to shift EV capacity back to ICE vehicles
- Geographic competition: Southern "battery belt" states (Georgia, Tennessee) are capturing EV investment that historically would have gone to Michigan
- Tariff uncertainty: 2025 tariffs proved marginally positive but policy remains fluid

**Non-consensus view on auto:** The EV transition job loss estimates (30-40% reduction) may be overstated. University of Michigan research shows plants transitioning to EV production actually increased assembly jobs up to 10x. The transition takes 15 years, giving communities time to adjust. This is more bullish than the conventional narrative suggests.

### Implications for Community Banking

1. **Loan demand:** Flat-to-declining population limits organic loan growth. Community banks must rely on market share gains or M&A.
2. **Deposit growth:** Same demographic constraints apply. Competition for deposits will remain intense.
3. **Credit quality:** Auto industry concentration creates tail risk. A severe auto downturn would cascade through the Michigan economy.

## Community Bank M&A: The Most Important Dynamic

### Deal Activity Surge

2025 was a breakout year for community bank M&A:
- 181 deals announced (highest since 2021)
- $47 billion total value
- 127 community bank acquisitions completed

### 2026 Outlook: Acceleration Expected

Multiple factors point to continued M&A acceleration:

1. **Regulatory tailwind:** OCC and FDIC rescinded Biden-era restrictive merger policies. Approval probability has materially increased.
2. **Aging bank owners:** Average community bank CEO age is 60+. Succession planning is driving sale decisions.
3. **Scale economics:** Compliance, technology, and deposit competition costs favor larger institutions.
4. **Earnings pressure:** Median selling bank ROAA declined to 0.6% in 2025, down from 0.8% in 2024. Underperforming banks are selling.

### Deal Multiples

- Median P/TBV: 146-147% (2025), highest since 2022 but below pre-pandemic levels
- Range: 110% (average seller) to 150-170% (strategic targets) to 200%+ (exceptional franchises)
- Pay-to-trade ratio: Buyers targeting <100%, limiting premiums for sellers trading above TBV

### What Makes a Community Bank Attractive for Acquisition

Based on 2025 deal patterns:
1. **Clean deposit franchise:** High noninterest-bearing deposits, low cost of funds, granular consumer deposits
2. **Manageable CRE concentration:** Particularly low office exposure
3. **Geographic fit:** In-market or adjacent-market acquisitions reduce integration risk
4. **Reasonable size:** $500M-$2B assets is the sweet spot. Smaller banks often not worth the integration cost; larger banks more expensive.
5. **Capable management:** Acquirers increasingly want to retain key talent

### Cycle Position Assessment

**Current Position: Early-to-Mid Cycle for M&A wave**

The 2025-2026 window is optimal for sellers. Friendly regulators, improving valuations, and strategic urgency are all aligned. By 2027-2028, the environment may shift as a new administration brings regulatory uncertainty.

## The One Industry Factor That Matters

**For Michigan community bank economics, the single most important variable is M&A takeout probability.**

Here's why this dominates:

1. **NIMs are stabilizing** at reasonable levels and are unlikely to drive significant earnings variance in either direction.
2. **Credit quality** is mature but manageable, with Michigan having better-than-average CRE exposure mix.
3. **Organic growth** is structurally limited by Michigan demographics.
4. **Operating leverage** is largely tapped out after years of cost-cutting.

The variance in outcomes for a small Michigan community bank comes down to:
- **Acquired:** Shareholders receive 140-170% of TBV in strategic premium
- **Not acquired:** Shareholders hold a low-growth, low-multiple stock indefinitely

**How to monitor:** Track community bank M&A deal flow, particularly in the Midwest. Watch for regulatory signals (FDIC, OCC statements on merger policy). Monitor aging owner demographics at target-size banks.

## What Consensus May Be Missing

### The Non-Consensus View: NIM Trough Is Behind Us

The market fears that Fed rate cuts will compress community bank NIMs significantly. This is the dominant narrative.

**Why consensus may be wrong:**

1. **Deposit beta asymmetry:** Banks are cutting deposit rates slowly while benefiting from fixed-rate asset repricing. The "cumulative deposit beta rose even after rate hikes stopped" dynamic works in reverse.

2. **CD maturity walls:** 2023-2024 vintage CDs at 5%+ are maturing into a 4% rate environment. This is an incremental NIM positive.

3. **Loan repricing momentum:** Loans originated in 2020-2021 at 3.5% are repricing at 6.5%+. This tailwind continues through 2026.

4. **Competition easing:** The post-SVB deposit panic created irrational competition. With stability returning, deposit pricing is normalizing.

**Evidence:** Community bank NIM rose 10bps in Q3 2025 despite Fed cuts. The Kansas City Fed notes community bank NIM is now above pre-pandemic averages.

**Implication:** If NIM troughs are behind us rather than ahead, community bank earnings estimates may be too pessimistic. This is modestly bullish for industry participants.

### Secondary Non-Consensus View: Michigan Auto Risk Is Overstated

The conventional wisdom is that Michigan's auto exposure creates unacceptable tail risk. But:
- EV transition job losses may be 10-15 year events, not cliff edges
- Some plants are actually adding jobs in EV transition
- Auto-related CRE in Michigan is industrial, not office, and is benefiting from nearshoring

This doesn't eliminate auto risk but suggests it's a longer-duration concern than typically assumed.

## Historical Context: What "Mid-Cycle" Means

For community banking, mid-cycle economics are characterized by:

- **NIM:** 3.50-3.75% (currently at 3.73%, within range)
- **ROAA:** 0.9-1.1% for well-run banks (industry varies widely)
- **CRE concentration:** 300-350% of capital (above this triggers regulatory scrutiny)
- **Deposit costs:** 1.5-2.0% cost of funds
- **Loan growth:** 3-5% annually (limited by demographics in Michigan)
- **Credit costs:** 15-30bps net charge-offs

Current conditions are approximately mid-cycle on most metrics, with NIM slightly above mid-cycle and credit costs slightly below (suggesting credit normalization ahead).

## Primary Industry Risks

1. **Recession triggering forced CRE recognition:** "Extend and pretend" unwinds simultaneously, creating capital stress for concentrated lenders.

2. **Michigan auto industry disruption:** Sudden (rather than gradual) job losses would cascade through regional economy.

3. **Deposit competition reignition:** Another SVB-type event or aggressive fintech competition could restart deposit wars.

4. **Regulatory reversal:** New administration in 2028 could reimpose restrictive merger policies, closing the M&A window.

5. **Interest rate surprise:** Either direction. Aggressive cuts (>3 in 2026) compress NIM faster; rate increases would pressure credit quality.

**Sanity Check:** If I knew nothing about any specific company in this industry, would industry economics alone make me cautious, comfortable, or excited about capital being deployed here?

**Answer:** Comfortable but not excited. Industry economics are roughly mid-cycle with modest tailwinds (NIM stability, M&A optionality) offsetting modest headwinds (limited growth, credit cycle maturation). The industry is not structurally attractive for organic growth, but M&A dynamics create potential premium realization for well-positioned participants.

For a potential M&A target specifically, industry conditions are favorable. Regulatory tailwinds, improving deal multiples, and buyer demand create a supportive environment for exits. The key question becomes company-specific: is this bank attractive enough to command a premium?
