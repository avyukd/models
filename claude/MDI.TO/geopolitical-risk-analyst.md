# Major Drilling Group International (MDI.TO) -- Geopolitical & Sovereign Risk Assessment

## Applicability

Geopolitical risk is material. MDI operates across Latin America, Africa, Asia-Pacific, and Mongolia with ~62% of fiscal 2025 revenue from outside Canada-US. Contract drilling for mining clients exposes the company to resource nationalism, permitting regimes, and currency volatility across multiple emerging market jurisdictions.

**Key country presences:** Canada, U.S., Chile, Peru, Argentina, Brazil, Colombia, Guyana Shield region, Australia, Mongolia, Indonesia, Tanzania, Botswana, Namibia, Mozambique.

**Critical shift in progress:** South & Central America grew 40% YoY in FY2025, driven by the Explomin acquisition (Peru-based) and Chilean copper exploration. In Q2 FY2026, South America generated $110.7M of $244.1M quarterly revenue (45%), surpassing Canada-US at $87.6M (36%). The geographic center of gravity is migrating toward Latin America.

**Key counterparties:** Major and mid-tier mining companies are MDI's customers, not governments directly. MDI does not hold mining concessions or licenses -- it drills on behalf of license holders.

## 2. Sovereign & Regime Risk Assessment

**Chile:** Stable democracy with strong rule of law. The 2023 Mining Royalty Law (effective January 2024) imposed an ad valorem component plus a margin-based tax reaching 46.5% for large copper producers. This is a cost on MDI's clients, not MDI directly, but higher royalties can reduce exploration budgets. Chile remains Latin America's most investable mining jurisdiction.

**Peru:** Chronic political instability -- six presidents in five years prior to current administration. However, mining remains constitutionally protected and economically critical (~10% of GDP). The Explomin acquisition concentrates MDI's growth bet here. Permitting delays are the primary risk, not nationalization.

**Argentina:** Milei's reforms have liberalized the economy, eased currency controls, and created a more mining-friendly regime than any period in recent decades. The RIGI (Large Investment Incentive Regime) provides tax stability guarantees for mining projects. Near-term trajectory is favorable but Argentine policy is structurally volatile across electoral cycles.

**Indonesia:** MDI's largest customer in Indonesia experienced an "operational incident" that impacted Q2 FY2026 Australasia-Africa revenue. Indonesia has a history of export bans (nickel ore ban, 2020) and periodic resource nationalist policy. Concentration risk around a single large customer in this jurisdiction is notable.

**Mongolia:** Mining-dependent economy with a track record of renegotiating foreign mining agreements (Oyu Tolgoi disputes with Rio Tinto). Rule of law is developing. MDI's exposure here appears modest but warrants monitoring.

**Africa (Tanzania, Botswana, Namibia, Mozambique):** Mixed risk profile. Botswana and Namibia are among Africa's most stable democracies. Tanzania has a history of retroactive tax claims against miners. Mozambique faces an active insurgency in Cabo Delgado. Overall African exposure appears small relative to other segments.

## 3. Regulatory & State Intervention Risk

MDI's business model provides a structural buffer against direct state intervention. As a contract driller, MDI does not own mining licenses, concessions, or mineral rights. The mechanisms of resource nationalism -- royalty increases, forced divestment, nationalization -- hit MDI's clients, not MDI itself. MDI's risk is second-order: if clients reduce exploration budgets due to regulatory burden, drilling demand falls.

The more direct risks are: (a) permitting delays that postpone drilling campaigns, and (b) environmental regulations restricting where and how drilling occurs. Both are tightening globally but apply uniformly to competitors.

## 4. Conflict & Sanctions Exposure

MDI has no material exposure to sanctioned jurisdictions (Russia, Iran, North Korea, etc.). No conflict zone exposure beyond peripheral Mozambique operations. No known defense or dual-use technology concerns. Sanctions risk is negligible.

## 5. Currency & Capital Controls

MDI reports in CAD but earns in USD, CLP (Chilean peso), PEN (Peruvian sol), ARS (Argentine peso), AUD, and various African currencies. Key dynamics:

- **Argentine peso:** Historically the most volatile. Milei's reforms have stabilized the FX regime, but ARS depreciation risk remains structural. Capital controls have been substantially eased.
- **Chilean peso / Peruvian sol:** Moderate volatility, generally convertible.
- **Canadian dollar weakness:** Actually benefits MDI since non-CAD revenues translate into more CAD.
- **Capital repatriation:** No current restrictions in key jurisdictions, but Argentina's history of capital controls makes this a standing risk.

MDI's natural FX diversification across 5+ currencies provides some offset, though the Latin American tilt increases EM currency exposure.

## 6. Scenario & Tail Risk Framing

**Base case:** Copper and gold exploration budgets remain strong, driving continued demand across MDI's geographies. Latin American growth continues. No material sovereign intervention.

**Adverse case:** Peru enters another political crisis and freezes mining permits for 6-12 months, directly impacting the Explomin-driven growth thesis. Chile raises royalties further, crimping client budgets. Combined impact: 15-25% revenue drag on the South American segment.

**Severe tail case:** A synchronized downturn in commodity prices coincides with resource nationalist policy shifts across multiple Latin American jurisdictions. Difficult to envision permanent capital loss given MDI's asset-light model (drilling rigs are mobile and redeployable), but earnings could compress sharply.

## 7. Mitigants & Offsets

- **Asset-light, mobile model:** Drilling rigs can be redeployed across jurisdictions. MDI does not own immovable mineral assets.
- **Geographic diversification:** Presence in 15+ countries means no single jurisdiction dominates.
- **No direct government counterparty risk:** Clients are mining companies, not sovereigns.
- **"Friendly jurisdiction" tailwind:** Western critical minerals strategies (Canada-Australia bilateral agreement, October 2025; U.S.-Australia framework) are channeling exploration spending toward jurisdictions where MDI has strong presence. Canada and Australia together host the majority of democratic-aligned mining companies, per ASPI analysis.
- **Counter-cyclical geographic mix:** When Latin America softens, Australia/Canada often strengthens, and vice versa.

## The One Geopolitical Risk That Matters

**Peru permitting and political instability.** MDI has made a concentrated growth bet on Peru via the Explomin acquisition. Peru's mining sector is economically critical but politically contested -- community opposition, environmental permitting bottlenecks, and chronic government instability can freeze exploration campaigns. If Peru's permitting environment deteriorates materially, the Explomin acquisition underperforms and the South American growth engine stalls. This is not a permanent capital loss risk (rigs are redeployable), but it is the primary threat to the growth thesis.

## What Consensus May Be Missing

The market likely views MDI's emerging market exposure as a generic risk factor. The non-consensus insight is that **MDI's business model structurally de-risks geographic exposure relative to mining operators.** MDI does not own concessions, does not face nationalization risk, and can redeploy assets. The entity bearing sovereign risk is the mining client, not MDI. This means MDI's emerging market discount should be smaller than what is applied to mining operators in the same jurisdictions. Additionally, the "friendly jurisdiction" critical minerals push by Western governments is a positive catalyst that benefits MDI's Canadian and Australian operations disproportionately -- exploration spending in these regions is policy-supported and rising. MDI's geographic diversification is more of a hidden advantage than a hidden risk, provided Peru does not become a concentrated point of failure.
