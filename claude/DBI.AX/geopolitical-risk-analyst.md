# DBI.AX -- Geopolitical & Sovereign Risk Assessment

**Date:** 2026-02-23 | **Price:** AUD 5.31

## Geographic Exposure Map

**Revenue geography:** 100% Australia (single asset -- Dalrymple Bay Terminal, Queensland). Revenue is the Terminal Infrastructure Charge (TIC), regulated by the Queensland Competition Authority (QCA), collected on 84.2Mtpa of fully contracted take-or-pay capacity.

**End-market geography (coal destinations, H1-24):** Japan, South Korea, India, Taiwan, China accounted for ~71% of throughput. India volumes +30% YoY. Coal shipped to 22 countries from 21 mines owned by 11 customers.

**Critical distinction:** DBI does not sell coal. It charges a per-tonne toll. Revenue is contractually decoupled from coal prices and destination mix. The geopolitical question is not "where does the coal go" but "will the mines keep producing and contracts keep renewing."

## The One Geopolitical Risk That Matters

**Contract renewal risk driven by structural decline in seaborne met coal demand post-2030.**

This is not a near-term risk. Contracts are 100% take-or-pay through June 2028, and the concession runs to 2051 (option to 2100). The terminal risk is that when contracts come up for renewal, the Bowen Basin coal complex faces declining global demand, mine closures (already visible -- BHP Mitsubishi Alliance cut 750 jobs and mothballed Saraji South in September 2025), and DBI cannot re-contract at current capacity levels.

The trigger mechanisms are convergent:
- **China:** Xi pledged coal use peaks by 2026. Domestic supply approaching self-sufficiency by 2030s. Australia's share of Chinese coal imports fell from ~40% to <10% post-ban, and has not recovered. Mongolia and Russia have structurally displaced Australian supply (per Lowy Institute, ASPI analysis).
- **India:** The consensus "India replaces China" thesis is challenged. India's Mission Coking Coal targets doubling domestic met coal production to 140Mtpa by 2030. Russia's share of Indian coking coal imports surged from 7% (2020) to 22% (2024). IEEFA explicitly argues India "will disappoint Australian miners."
- **Technology:** Electric arc furnace steelmaking and hydrogen-based DRI reduce met coal intensity per tonne of steel. This is a slow-moving but directionally clear structural shift.

**Impact if contracts cannot be renewed at current volumes:** The regulated TIC model means DBI earns a return on its asset base. If contracted capacity falls materially below 84.2Mtpa, the revenue base shrinks proportionally. The 2051 concession becomes less valuable. This is a terminal value question, not an earnings question.

## Sovereign & Regulatory Risk

**Jurisdiction quality:** Australia is a rule-of-law democracy with strong property rights, independent judiciary, and bilateral investment treaty network. Expropriation risk is negligible. This is a major mitigant relative to resource infrastructure in emerging markets.

**QCA regulation:** The QCA regulates DBI as a monopoly infrastructure provider, setting the TIC tariff. The QCA rejected DBI's proposal to deregulate pricing and will continue setting tariffs until at least 2030. This caps upside but provides earnings predictability. The regulatory framework is the dominant sovereign risk -- not expropriation, but margin compression through regulatory decisions.

**Queensland coal royalties:** The progressive royalty regime (7% at <$100/t, up to 40% at >$300/t) applies to miners, not DBI directly. However, high royalties reduce mine economics, accelerating closures and potentially reducing future contracted volumes. The Queensland government maintained the royalty structure despite industry pressure and job losses. This is an indirect but real transmission mechanism.

**Safeguard Mechanism:** Australia's emissions reduction framework applies to large industrial facilities. Coal terminals are not primary targets, but tightening baselines create incremental compliance costs. The scheme review in 2026-27 could expand scope. Judgment: minor cost risk, not existential.

## Conflict & Sanctions Exposure

**Direct exposure: Nil.** DBI has no operations in sanctioned jurisdictions. Its customers are Australian miners, not foreign entities.

**Indirect exposure:** A severe deterioration in Australia-China relations (repeat of 2020-22 coal ban) would reduce throughput at DBT. However, the take-or-pay structure means DBI still gets paid on contracted volumes regardless. The miners bear the volume and price risk. The 2020-22 China ban is the relevant precedent: Australian coal was redirected to India, Japan, Korea. DBI's throughput was not materially disrupted. This is an important empirical data point that the market may already understand.

## Currency & Capital Controls

Immaterial. Single-jurisdiction AUD-denominated asset. No FX exposure, no repatriation risk, no capital controls risk.

## Scenario Framework

**Base case:** Contracts roll at or near current capacity through 2028+. TIC grows with CPI and NECAP additions (~$30-50M/year capex). Planned expansion to 99.1Mtpa provides growth optionality. Stable regulated income.

**Adverse case:** Post-2028, contract renewals decline as Bowen Basin mines close due to low met coal prices and Queensland royalty burden. Contracted capacity falls 15-25% by early 2030s. TIC revenue declines proportionally. Concession value impaired.

**Severe tail case:** Accelerated global steel decarbonization combined with mine closures reduces Bowen Basin output below 50Mtpa by late 2030s. DBI becomes a stranded infrastructure asset well before 2051 concession expiry. Permanent capital loss.

## Mitigants & Offsets

- Take-or-pay contracts eliminate near-term volume risk (through June 2028)
- Australian rule-of-law jurisdiction eliminates seizure/expropriation risk
- Met coal (majority of throughput) has longer demand runway than thermal coal
- Potential capacity expansion to 99.1Mtpa suggests customer demand exists near-term
- Infrastructure monopoly with no competitive bypass risk at Hay Point
- Cost pass-through structure protects margins

## What Consensus May Be Missing

The market likely overweights the "energy transition kills coal infrastructure" narrative for DBI specifically, and underweights the structural protection of take-or-pay contracts and regulated returns. DBI is not a coal miner -- it is a toll road. The 2020-22 China ban demonstrated that DBI's revenue is resilient to destination disruption.

However, consensus may also be underweighting the post-2030 contract renewal risk. The "India replaces China" thesis for Australian met coal is weaker than commonly assumed. Russia's rapid displacement of Australian supply into India (from 7% to 22% market share in four years) and India's domestic production ambitions suggest the demand runway is shorter than the 2051 concession implies. The question is whether the current ~6% dividend yield adequately compensates for a concession that may have materially less value in its later decades.

## Final Classification

**Geopolitical Risk Level:** Low-to-Moderate

**Primary Risk Vector:** Structural demand decline reducing contract renewal prospects post-2028

**Tail Risk Severity:** Material (not existential near-term, but potential permanent value impairment in 2030s)

**Key Monitoring Signals:**
- Bowen Basin mine closure announcements and production guidance
- India's domestic met coal production progress vs. Mission Coking Coal targets
- Russia's share of Indian/Asian coking coal imports
- QCA regulatory decisions on TIC methodology and deregulation
- DBI contract renewal announcements and capacity utilization post-2028
- Queensland state election outcomes and royalty policy changes
