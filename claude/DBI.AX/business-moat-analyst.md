# DBI.AX -- Business Quality & Moat Analysis

## Business Model Summary

DBI owns a 99-year lease (50+49 option) on the Dalrymple Bay Terminal (DBT), the world's largest metallurgical coal export terminal at 85Mtpa nameplate capacity, located in Queensland's Bowen Basin. Revenue comes from a single regulated tariff -- the Terminal Infrastructure Charge (TIC) -- paid per contracted tonne on a take-or-pay basis. The QCA (Queensland Competition Authority) regulates tariffs based on a Regulated Asset Base (RAB) model, allowing a regulated return on capital plus pass-through of non-expansion capital (NECAP) costs. FY25 EBITDA was A$294M on ~A$796M revenue. The cost structure is heavily fixed -- operating a physical terminal with minimal volume-driven variable costs.

This is a toll-road business. DBI does not take commodity price risk on coal. It collects fees per tonne regardless of whether coal actually ships (take-or-pay).

## Source(s) of Advantage

**Regulated monopoly infrastructure with geographic lock-in.** DBT is the only third-party-access coal terminal serving the central Bowen Basin. The adjacent Hay Point Coal Terminal (HPCT) is owned/operated by BHP-Mitsubishi Alliance (BMA) and serves only BMA mines -- it is not available to other producers. Bowen Basin miners requiring export capacity have no alternative terminal without massive rail infrastructure investment to reach Gladstone or Abbot Point, both of which serve different basins. This is a "cannot switch" dynamic, not a "will not switch" dynamic.

**Take-or-pay contracts eliminate volume risk.** 100% of 84.2Mtpa capacity is contracted take-or-pay through June 2028 with evergreen renewal options. Users pay whether they ship or not. This is structural -- the QCA access undertaking mandates this framework, and miners need the capacity option to satisfy their own offtake obligations.

**RAB-based tariff provides inflation linkage.** The TIC has escalated consistently: A$2.46/t (TY-20/21) to A$3.02 (TY-21/22) to A$3.59 (TY-24/25) to A$3.72 (TY-25/26). The base TIC component inflates annually. NECAP additions (~A$30-50M/year) grow the asset base, which in turn grows the allowable tariff. This is a self-reinforcing mechanism: maintenance capex increases the RAB, which increases tariffs, which increases revenue.

**Regulatory framework as both shield and ceiling.** QCA regulation prevents DBI from extracting monopoly rents, but it also prevents political or customer pressure from compressing returns below the regulated rate. The access undertaking was extended to July 2031. This provides a decade of framework certainty.

## Competitive Landscape

There are no direct competitors for DBT's customer base. The terminal is a natural monopoly -- a second terminal at this location would be uneconomic, and rail links lock Bowen Basin producers into Hay Point port. HPCT (BMA-owned) is captive. Abbot Point and Gladstone terminals serve geographically distinct mining regions.

**The relevant competitive question is not "who competes with DBT?" but "what substitutes exist for Bowen Basin met coal exports?"** The answer today: effectively none. This is infrastructure with no bypass risk. A new terminal would require billions in capital and a decade of approvals. No rational competitor would build one given the regulated return framework caps upside.

## Customer Behavior & Revenue Quality

**Customer concentration is moderate.** The Bowen Basin has multiple large met coal producers (BHP, Glencore, Anglo American, Stanmore, Whitehaven post-acquisition). No single customer dominates contracted capacity to a dangerous degree given the take-or-pay structure -- even if one miner goes bankrupt, the contract obligations persist through the asset.

**Retention is structural, not behavioral.** Customers renew because there is no alternative, not because of service quality or relationship. Evergreen renewal provisions make contract rollover the default. Churn risk is near zero absent mine closures.

**Revenue visibility is exceptionally high.** Contracted capacity x TIC = revenue, adjusted for actual throughput above contracted minimums. The take-or-pay floor means revenue is knowable years in advance.

## Evidence of Moat in the Financials

EBITDA of A$294M on A$796M revenue implies ~37% EBITDA margins -- modest by infrastructure standards because revenue includes pass-through operating costs (DBI reports revenue gross of terminal operating costs paid to the operator, DBCT Management). Funds from operations of A$173M growing at ~10.6% YoY reflects the RAB growth mechanism working.

Margin stability is embedded in the regulatory structure. The TIC is set to provide a regulated return on the RAB. Unless the QCA changes the allowed return methodology, margins are structurally stable. This is not a competitive moat visible in margins -- it is a regulatory construct. The distinction matters: margin stability here reflects regulatory protection, not competitive superiority.

Distribution payout ratio above 100% of statutory net income (131%) is typical for infrastructure trusts where depreciation exceeds maintenance capex. Not a red flag in isolation but requires monitoring of actual NECAP spend vs. RAB depreciation.

## Fragility Analysis

**Met coal demand secular decline.** DBT is 100% exposed to metallurgical coal. Steel decarbonization (electric arc furnaces, hydrogen-based DRI) could structurally reduce seaborne met coal demand over 15-30 years. This is the terminal risk to the thesis. The 99-year lease is only valuable if coal ships for decades.

**Interest rate exposure.** DBI carries significant debt. Weighted all-in interest rate steps from ~4.63% to ~6.5% when 2021-vintage swaps roll off mid-2026. Higher structural rates compress distributable cash flow without affecting the RAB return. The regulated return should adjust over time, but with lag.

**Regulatory risk is real but bounded.** If QCA reduces the allowed return on the RAB, DBI's earnings power falls directly. The 2031 access undertaking extension provides medium-term protection. Post-2031 is uncertain.

**Queensland political risk.** The state government owns the land and grants the lease. Policy changes toward coal (royalties, environmental restrictions) could indirectly impair terminal economics by reducing mine viability.

**8X expansion risk.** The planned expansion to 99.1Mtpa requires significant capital. If demand doesn't materialize, DBI could overbuild. However, the take-or-pay structure and customer-funded NECAP model mitigate this -- expansion requires customer commitment before proceeding.

## Moat & Durability Classification

**Moat Strength: Strong.** Geographic monopoly on critical export infrastructure with no bypass risk, regulated tariff framework, and 100% take-or-pay contracting. This is as close to a natural monopoly as publicly listed infrastructure gets.

**Earnings Durability: Medium.** The regulatory and contractual framework is robust through 2028-2031. Beyond that, the terminal risk is secular met coal demand decline. Earnings are highly durable over 5-10 years; the 20-30 year picture depends entirely on steel industry decarbonization pace.

## The One Thing That Matters for Durability

The longevity of seaborne metallurgical coal demand. Every other risk -- regulation, contracts, competition -- is well-managed. But DBI is a single-commodity, single-asset infrastructure play. If global steel production shifts meaningfully away from blast furnace (coking coal) toward EAF/DRI, throughput declines and take-or-pay contracts become unenforceable against bankrupt miners. The moat is impregnable against competitors but vulnerable to the commodity it serves becoming obsolete.

## What Consensus May Be Missing

**Bearish insight the bulls may underweight:** The RAB growth mechanism creates an illusion of compounding. NECAP additions grow the asset base and tariff, but this is maintenance capital being relabeled as growth. DBI is spending A$30-50M/year on NECAP that gets added to the RAB, inflating the tariff. This is not organic growth -- it is regulatory accounting that converts capex into revenue. When the terminal ages further, NECAP requirements will increase, but the underlying productive capacity does not. Bulls treating DBI's 3-7% distribution growth target as organic compounding are conflating maintenance spend with value creation.

**Bullish insight the bears may underweight:** Met coal demand destruction is consensus, but the timeline matters enormously. India and Southeast Asia are building blast furnace capacity now. Seaborne met coal trade may actually increase over the next decade before declining. DBT's geographic position serving Asian markets and its status as the world's largest met coal terminal means it will be among the last assets to see volume decline -- the most efficient, lowest-cost export infrastructure gets decommissioned last.
