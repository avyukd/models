# Electrical Infrastructure Services: Industry Structure & Cycle Analysis

**Date:** 2026-02-26

---

## Industry Economics: What Drives Profitability

Electrical contracting is a labor-intensive, project-based business. The NECA average sales dollar breaks down as: materials 31%, direct labor 30%, labor burden 13%, overhead 13%, net profit ~3% (per NECA). This 3% net margin is the historical norm for the industry at large -- the figure that should anchor any "mid-cycle" assessment.

The industry has historically been low-margin, fragmented, and cyclical. What is happening now is structurally different in ways that matter, but also in ways the market may be over-extrapolating.

## Demand Drivers: Separating Structural from Cyclical

| Driver | Nature | Magnitude | Duration |
|--------|--------|-----------|----------|
| Data center buildout (AI/cloud) | Structural | Data center power demand to nearly triple by 2030 (per S&P Global); hyperscaler capex >$200B in 2024, +62% YoY | Multi-year but with potential air pockets |
| Grid modernization | Structural | US electricity demand growing again after a decade of flatness (per EIA); 140 GW new generation capacity needed by 2030 | Decade+ |
| IIJA/IRA/CHIPS spending | Cyclical-structural hybrid | >$700B authorized but only 1% outlayed by DOE as of year-end 2024; Trump administration funding pauses add uncertainty | Uncertain -- disbursement has been extremely slow |
| Reshoring/manufacturing | Structural | CHIPS Act fabs, battery plants, LNG | 5-10 year wave |
| Residential housing | Cyclical | IESC residential segment down 6.1% in FY2025 | Rate-sensitive |

The critical insight: data centers and electrification are not cyclical demand -- they represent a structural step-change in electrical infrastructure needs. US electricity consumption is forecast to grow 25% by 2030 after a decade of near-zero growth (per EIA, Grid Strategies). This is the most significant demand inflection the electrical contracting industry has seen since post-WWII electrification.

## Supply Side: The Binding Constraint

The industry's supply response is severely constrained by labor:

- BLS projects 81,000 electrician openings annually through 2034
- 90% of construction firms reported difficulty filling skilled positions in 2024
- Electrician workforce projected to shrink 14% by 2030 while demand could increase 25% (per BLS, NECA)
- Training pipeline (4-5 year apprenticeships) cannot scale quickly

**This is the single most important structural fact about this industry.** Unlike commodity industries where capital can create new supply in 18-24 months, electrical labor supply takes 4-5 years to develop and is shrinking in absolute terms. This fundamentally changes the industry's margin profile.

## Cyclicality Assessment: Historical Context

| Period | What Happened | Margin Impact |
|--------|---------------|---------------|
| 2008-2009 | Industry revenue fell >20%; Top 50 contractors down 4.5% (per EC&M) | Severe margin compression as firms competed for scarce work |
| 2010-2015 | Slow recovery, retrofit-heavy workload | Margins remained depressed |
| 2016-2019 | Steady growth, commercial construction recovery | Margins normalized |
| 2020 | COVID disruption, temporary shutdown | Brief dip, rapid recovery |
| 2021-present | Unprecedented demand acceleration | Margins expanded materially |

Historical industry net margins of ~3% (NECA average) versus the 10%+ operating margins now being achieved by scaled players represent a massive deviation from historical norms. The question is whether this deviation is cyclical or structural.

## Current Cycle Position

**Assessment: Late cycle on volume, but structural repricing of labor scarcity has not yet been fully recognized.**

The evidence:
- Revenue growth rates of 17-21% YoY for scaled players are not sustainable at this pace
- Backlogs are at record levels across the industry
- Federal infrastructure spending remains mostly un-disbursed, representing future demand not yet in the numbers
- Labor constraints mean this demand cannot be easily absorbed, supporting pricing

**Confidence level: Moderate.** The biggest uncertainty is whether AI/data center capex sustains or faces a correction similar to past tech investment cycles (fiber in 2000-2001, for instance).

## Mid-Cycle Definition

"Normal" for this industry historically meant: 3-5% net margins, GDP-linked demand growth, moderate competition for projects, adequate (if tight) labor supply.

The new mid-cycle -- if structural demand shifts are real -- would look like: 6-8% net margins for scaled players, demand growth of 5-8% annually, persistent labor scarcity supporting pricing discipline, and a shift toward higher-complexity work (data centers, power infrastructure) that favors larger, more capable firms.

## The One Industry Factor That Matters

**Skilled electrical labor supply is the binding constraint that determines everything else.**

If labor were abundant, the margin expansion story collapses -- more competitors could bid on work, pricing discipline erodes, and margins revert to historical 3% norms. But labor is not abundant, cannot become abundant quickly (4-5 year apprenticeship cycles), and the demographic trend (retirements) is working against supply growth.

Monitor: BLS monthly data on electrician employment levels, NECA workforce surveys, apprenticeship enrollment data. An inflection in labor supply growth would be the leading indicator of margin mean-reversion.

## What Consensus May Be Missing

**Consensus treats this as a "data center cycle play." The variant view: this is actually a labor scarcity story, and the market is underpricing how long elevated margins can persist.**

Most analysts frame the thesis around demand (data centers, electrification, infrastructure bills). But demand is only half the equation. The supply side -- specifically, the inability to rapidly scale skilled electrical labor -- is what converts demand strength into margin expansion. Even if data center spending pauses or moderates, the structural labor shortage means margins are unlikely to revert to historical 3% norms anytime soon.

The bearish variant to watch: immigration policy changes that dramatically increase construction labor supply, or a deep recession that destroys demand faster than supply adjusts. The 2008-2009 precedent (revenue -20%) shows this industry is not immune to macro downturns.

**A second non-consensus risk:** the Trump administration's freeze on IIJA/IRA disbursements. Only 1% of DOE-allocated funds had been outlayed as of year-end 2024. If these funds are permanently redirected, a significant chunk of expected future demand evaporates. Most industry forecasts assume this spending eventually flows -- that assumption deserves more scrutiny. *This is bearish.*
