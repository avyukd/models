# SNAP (Snap Inc.) -- Management & Capital Allocation Assessment

**Stock Price:** ~$5.07 (Feb 25, 2026). Market Cap: ~$8.6B. 52-week range: $4.65-$10.59.

---

## 1. Management Overview

- **Evan Spiegel** -- Co-Founder, CEO. Holds Class C shares representing ~88.5% of total voting power (with Bobby Murphy). Class A public shareholders have zero votes. Total reported compensation ~$3.3M, structured as 100% bonus/equity, 0% salary.
- **Bobby Murphy** -- Co-Founder, CTO. Same Class C voting control as Spiegel.
- Spiegel has executed 64 insider sales since 2021. Recent sales: 1.39M shares ($10M) in Aug 2025, 1.26M shares ($8/share) Dec 2025, 1.22M shares ($10M) Jan 2026. Consistent, programmatic selling with no insider purchases identified.

## 2. Incentive Structure & Alignment

**Governance is the story.** Snap issued only non-voting Class A shares at IPO. Spiegel and Murphy hold Class C shares (10 votes each), controlling ~88.5% of votes. Public shareholders cannot vote on executive compensation, board composition, or anything else that matters. The founders approved all 2025 annual meeting proposals via written consent before the meeting occurred.

This is not a dual-class structure with some minority protection. This is absolute control with zero accountability to outside shareholders. There is no mechanism -- none -- for shareholders to discipline management. No say-on-pay. No proxy fight. No activist intervention possible.

**What behavior does this encourage?** Whatever Spiegel wants to do. If he wants to spend $3B over 11 years on AR glasses that generate no revenue, he can. If he wants to spin that into a subsidiary (Specs Inc., formed Jan 2026) and raise outside capital for it, he can. No board or shareholder can stop him.

**Who benefits if the stock underperforms?** Management still collects SBC that vests regardless of stock performance. Spiegel's voting power is unaffected by price. The only "risk" is that his equity value declines -- but he is already a programmatic seller.

## 3. Stock-Based Compensation Discipline

This is SNAP's defining capital allocation issue.

| Year | Revenue | SBC | SBC % of Revenue | Diluted Shares |
|------|---------|-----|-------------------|----------------|
| 2022 | $4.60B | ~$1.5B | ~33% | 1.608B |
| 2023 | $4.61B | ~$1.3B | ~28% | 1.613B |
| 2024 | $5.36B | $1.04B | ~19% | 1.659B |
| 2025 | $5.93B | ~$1.1B (est) | ~19% | 1.695B |

SBC has declined as a percentage of revenue from extreme levels (~33%) to merely very high levels (~19%). In dollar terms, SBC has been $1-1.5B annually. For context, FY2025 free cash flow was $437M -- meaning SBC runs at roughly 2.5x free cash flow. Snap is issuing more in stock compensation than it generates in cash.

**Share count trajectory:** Diluted shares grew from 1.608B (2022) to 1.695B (2025) -- a net increase of ~87M shares (5.4%) over three years despite $1.5B+ in cumulative buybacks ($1B in 2022, $189M in 2023, $311M in 2024). The buybacks are not reducing the share count. They are partially offsetting dilution, and failing even at that.

The company itself admits this. The Feb 2026 $500M buyback authorization was described as intended to "offset a portion of the dilution related to the issuance of restricted stock units." Not to return capital. Not to shrink the float. Just to slow the bleeding.

**SBC is not treated as a real cost.** Snap reports "Adjusted EBITDA" that excludes SBC, which flatters profitability. On a GAAP basis including SBC, the company remains unprofitable.

## 4. Capital Allocation Track Record

**Reinvestment:** $3B spent over 11 years on AR/Spectacles with no meaningful revenue to show for it. The latest move: spinning out the AR business into Specs Inc. (Jan 2026) to seek outside funding. This is either a belated recognition that Snap shareholders should not solely fund speculative hardware, or a way to obscure continued spending. The consumer launch is targeted for 2026, with no clear path to profitability.

Core ad business R&D is harder to isolate, but revenue growth has decelerated to 11% in FY2025 from 16% in FY2024. Returns on core reinvestment appear adequate but not exceptional.

**M&A:** Not a major capital deployment channel. No large acquisitions in recent years. This is a positive -- Spiegel has not engaged in empire-building through deals.

**Buybacks & Issuance:** As detailed above, buybacks are a treadmill. $1.5B+ spent since 2022, share count still up 5.4%. The $500M new authorization (Feb 2026) at current prices (~$5) could retire ~100M shares, but SBC will issue more than that. Net dilution will likely continue.

**Debt:** Snap issued $700M in junk bonds (Feb 2025). The company holds $2.9B in cash/securities, so the balance sheet is not distressed, but taking on high-yield debt while running a cash-consuming SBC program is aggressive. Debt is being used to fund operations and buybacks that offset dilution from compensation -- a circular structure that transfers value from bondholders and shareholders to employees.

## 5. Learning vs. Repetition

**Limited evidence of learning.** SBC as a percentage of revenue has improved, but this is largely a function of revenue growth rather than SBC discipline -- dollar SBC has stayed roughly flat at $1-1.1B. The Specs Inc. spinout could represent learning (stop subsidizing hardware from the ad business), or it could be financial engineering to hide costs. Spiegel's programmatic selling has been consistent for years -- no change in behavior despite stock declining 52% in the past year.

## 6. Forward-Looking Capital Allocation Risk

- SBC will likely remain $1.1-1.2B annually (per 2026 guidance). With FCF of ~$437M, the equity-to-cash compensation ratio remains deeply unfavorable to shareholders.
- Specs Inc. could become a capital sink if outside funding falls short. Snap may end up subsidizing it again.
- The $500M buyback will partially offset dilution but will not shrink the share count.
- No mechanism exists for shareholders to change any of this. Spiegel has absolute control.

---

## Final Classification

- **Incentive Alignment:** Weak. Zero shareholder voting rights. CEO is a programmatic seller. SBC dwarfs FCF.
- **Capital Allocation Discipline:** Low. $3B on AR with no returns. Buybacks that fail to offset dilution. Junk debt issuance.
- **Dilution Risk:** High. Net share count rising despite $1.5B+ in buybacks. SBC at ~19% of revenue and ~250% of FCF.
- **Primary Risk:** Spiegel's unchecked control means capital can be deployed into speculative ventures (AR hardware, new initiatives) indefinitely with no shareholder recourse, while SBC systematically transfers value from shareholders to employees.

## The One Thing That Determines Value Creation

Evan Spiegel's absolute voting control (88.5%) combined with zero say-on-pay means that all capital allocation is a bet on one person's judgment with no error-correction mechanism. If Spiegel allocates well, shareholders benefit. If he does not, there is no board, activist, or vote that can intervene. The stock's 52% decline over the past year while insiders sell programmatically suggests the market is beginning to price this governance discount -- but may not be pricing it fully given the structural permanence of the arrangement.

## What Consensus May Be Missing

The consensus narrative frames SNAP's SBC problem as "improving" because SBC/revenue has declined from ~33% to ~19%. This misses the point. In dollar terms, SBC has been flat at ~$1B+ while the company generates only ~$437M in FCF. Snap is paying employees 2.5x what the business generates in cash, funding the gap with stock dilution and junk debt. The buyback program is explicitly described as offsetting dilution, not returning capital. Every dollar of buybacks is a transfer from the balance sheet to employees, not to shareholders. The improving percentage masks the reality that shareholders are on a treadmill where their ownership is continuously diluted and buybacks merely slow the pace. Until SBC drops below FCF -- which requires either a dramatic revenue acceleration or SBC cuts that could trigger talent departures -- the equity is a melting ice cube.
