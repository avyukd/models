# Advanced Plastics Recycling Industry: Structure and Cycle Analysis

*Industry context for Aduro Clean Technologies (ADUR)*

---

## Executive Summary

The advanced plastics recycling industry (chemical recycling, including pyrolysis, depolymerization, and solvolysis) is a **pre-commercial, emerging sector** currently experiencing a brutal shakeout phase. Despite $50+ billion in projected investment needs by 2030 and powerful regulatory tailwinds (EU PPWR mandates 30-65% recycled content by 2030), the industry has failed to prove commercial viability at scale. The 2024-2025 period has been marked by high-profile bankruptcies (Brightmark, BlueCycle), plant closures (Ioniqa, Agilyx/Styrenyx, New Hope Energy), and over 50% of scheduled 2025 capacity missing deadlines.

The fundamental industry economics problem: **virgin plastic remains cheaper than recycled in most markets**, feedstock acquisition is operationally challenging, and technology risk has proven higher than promoters claimed. This is NOT a mature industry experiencing a cyclical trough - it is a nascent industry still searching for commercial proof-of-concept.

## Industry Structure Assessment

### What Industry Is This, Really?

This is critical: advanced plastics recycling is NOT a recycling industry in the traditional sense. It is:

1. **A technology licensing/development business** (pre-revenue phase)
2. **A specialty chemical processing business** (at commercial scale, if achieved)
3. **A regulatory arbitrage play** (value exists only because regulations mandate recycled content)

The industry has no meaningful revenues at scale. Per Lux Research, global advanced recycling ended 2024 at just under 1 million tonnes/year of installed capacity - trivial relative to ~400 million tonnes of annual global plastic production.

### Structural Classification

| Dimension | Assessment |
|-----------|------------|
| **Industry Trajectory** | Embryonic / Pre-Commercial |
| **Competitive Structure** | Fragmented, with many technology approaches competing |
| **Barriers to Entry** | Low for technology claims, high for commercial proof |
| **Capital Intensity** | Very High ($50-150M+ per commercial facility) |
| **Winner-Take-All Dynamics** | Unlikely - multiple technologies may coexist for different feedstocks |

### Technology Landscape

Three main approaches compete:

**1. Mechanical Recycling (Incumbent)**
- Handles 90%+ of current plastic recycling
- Economics proven but degradation limits recyclability
- Key player: PureCycle (PCT) - polypropylene focus
- PureCycle spent 2024 fighting operational problems at Ironton facility; only achieved 67% uptime by December 2024

**2. Pyrolysis (Dominant Advanced Recycling Approach)**
- Thermal decomposition at 400-700C
- Produces pyrolysis oil for petrochemical feedstock
- Major issues: energy intensive, contamination sensitivity, inconsistent output quality
- High-profile failures: Brightmark (5% capacity utilization, bankruptcy), Encina ($1.1B project canceled), multiple 2024-2025 closures

**3. Solvolysis/Depolymerization (Emerging)**
- Chemical breakdown using solvents or catalysts
- HCT (Hydrochemolytic Technology) falls here - water-based, lower temperature
- Claimed advantages: handles contaminated feedstock, lower energy, no hydrogen needed
- Commercial proof: None at scale

## Industry Economics: The Fundamental Problem

### The Virgin vs. Recycled Price Equation

The industry's entire economics depend on recycled plastic commanding a premium over virgin. Current reality:

| Region | Virgin HDPE ($/tonne) | Recycled HDPE ($/tonne) | Recycled Premium |
|--------|----------------------|------------------------|------------------|
| US | ~$943 | ~$1,631 | +73% (uneconomic for most buyers) |
| Europe | ~1,444 EUR | Comparable or higher | Near parity |
| China | Lower virgin | Higher recycled | Inverted (recycled more expensive) |

Per IEEFA research: buyers tolerate 10-20% recycled premiums, but at 30%+ premium "cost sensitivity substitution" occurs - they switch back to virgin.

**The structural problem:** Virgin plastic prices are driven by global petrochemical supply (currently oversupplied - hence cheap). Recycled prices are driven by collection, sorting, processing costs (relatively fixed). When virgin prices collapse (as in 2023-2024 due to petrochemical oversupply), recycled becomes uncompetitive.

### Chemical Recycling Unit Economics

Per academic literature and industry sources:

| Parameter | Range | Notes |
|-----------|-------|-------|
| Feedstock cost | $0-110/tonne | Above $110/tonne requires subsidies |
| Break-even pyrolysis oil price | 418-1,182 EUR/Mg | Scale dependent (100kt vs 3.75kt) |
| Typical margins (claimed) | 10-30% | Only achievable with subsidies/gate fees |
| Capacity utilization | 5-67% | Brightmark at 5%; PureCycle peaked at 67% |
| Payback period (claimed) | 3-5 years | Unproven at commercial scale |

**The scale paradox:** Small plants have uneconomic unit costs; large plants cannot source consistent feedstock. Academic studies show profitability only at 35kt/year+ scale with feedstock costs under $88/tonne - conditions rarely met.

### Why Plants Keep Failing

1. **Feedstock quality/consistency** - Collection systems don't deliver sorted, clean plastic at scale
2. **Technology risk** - Pyrolysis reactors foul; yields disappoint; maintenance costs exceed projections
3. **Output quality** - Pyrolysis oil often requires expensive upgrading for cracker feedstock
4. **Capital costs** - $100M+ facilities with 3-5 year construction timelines
5. **Competition from virgin** - Oil price volatility makes virgin plastic cheaper than projected

## Regulatory Tailwinds: The Demand Driver

### EU Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR)

Effective February 2025, with key dates:

| Deadline | Requirement |
|----------|-------------|
| 2026 | General application begins |
| 2030 | All packaging must be recyclable; 30-65% recycled content by category |
| 2035 | All packaging must be recycled at scale |
| 2040 | Recycled content requirements increase to 50-65% |

**Specific 2030 recycled content mandates:**
- 30% for contact-sensitive PET packaging (50% by 2040)
- 30% for single-use plastic beverage bottles (65% by 2040)
- 35% for other plastic packaging (65% by 2040)

### US Regulatory Environment

Federal mandates are weak:
- EPA Safer Choice: 15% minimum recycled content (voluntary program)
- Federal procurement: No single-use plastic by 2035

State-level action is stronger:
- California: 25% recycled content for beverage containers (2025)
- 7 states with EPR rules: CA, CO, ME, MD, MN, OR, WA

### Corporate Commitments

Major brand owners (Unilever, Nestle, P&G, etc.) have pledged recycled content targets. However, per 2025 reports, "some companies are retreating from those promises" as 2025 deadlines loom and economics remain unfavorable.

## Capacity and Demand Projections

### Installed Capacity (2024)

- Global advanced recycling: ~1 million tonnes/year
- 2025 expansion: >50% of scheduled projects expected to miss deadlines (per Lux Research)
- Delayed inflection point: Expected by 2025, now pushed to 2027-2028

### Market Size Projections

| Source | 2024 Value | 2034 Projection | CAGR |
|--------|-----------|-----------------|------|
| Chemical Recycling (broad) | $815M | $18.5B | 36.1% |
| Plastic Chemical Recycling | $249M | $1.6B | 21.2% |
| Advanced Recycling | $2.1B (2025) | $7.3B (2035) | 13.2% |

**Skeptical note:** These projections assume commercial scale-up that has not yet occurred. Every major advanced recycling forecast of the past 5 years has been revised downward.

### The Capacity Paradox

Per APR 2025 survey: US/Canada mechanical recyclers have **2 billion pounds of excess annual capacity** already. The constraint is not recycling capacity - it is:
1. Feedstock availability and quality
2. Demand for recycled content (brand commitment follow-through)
3. Price competitiveness vs. virgin

## Competitive Landscape

### Technology Approaches Comparison

| Technology | Advantages | Disadvantages | Commercial Status |
|------------|------------|---------------|-------------------|
| **Mechanical** | Proven economics, low energy | Quality degradation, limited resin types | Commercial |
| **Pyrolysis** | Handles mixed waste, oil output | Energy intensive, contamination sensitive, high failure rate | Pre-commercial, struggling |
| **HCT (Aduro)** | Low temp/pressure, handles contamination, no H2, 90% liquid yield (claimed) | Unproven at scale, pre-revenue | Pilot stage |
| **Depolymerization** | Monomer recovery, virgin-equivalent output | Resin-specific, complex chemistry | Limited commercial |

### Key Players and Status

- PureCycle (PCT): $224M net loss in first 9 months 2024; Ironton facility operational challenges; raised $300M in June 2025

**Pyrolysis:**

- Encina: Canceled $1.1B Pennsylvania project
- New Hope Energy: Shut down 2024
- Agilyx/Styrenyx: Shut down 2024

**Solvolysis/Emerging:**
- Aduro Clean Technologies: Pilot stage; HCT technology; partnership discussions with Brightlands Chemelot
- Ioniqa: Shut down 2024
- BlueCycle: Bankruptcy Q1 2025

## Where Are We in the Industry Cycle?

### This Is Not a Traditional Cycle

Standard cycle analysis assumes a mature industry with established economics experiencing temporary supply-demand imbalances. **That framework does not apply here.**

This industry is in the **technology development / commercial proof-of-concept phase**:

| Phase | Characteristics | Current Status |
|-------|----------------|----------------|
| Lab/Pilot | Technology works in controlled conditions | Passed for most technologies |
| Demonstration | Scaled up with real feedstocks | Many stuck here |
| First Commercial | Single commercial-scale plant operating | Very few (mostly struggling) |
| Commercial Scale-up | Replication with consistent economics | Not achieved by any advanced recycling tech |
| Industry Maturity | Predictable economics, cycle dynamics | Distant (10+ years) |

### Cycle Position Assessment

| Traditional Cycle Framework | Assessment |
|---------------------------|------------|
| Industry Cyclicality | N/A - Industry not mature enough to exhibit cycles |
| Current Position | "Demo/First Commercial" phase with high failure rate |
| Comparable Historical Analog | Early solar (2000s), early EV batteries (2010s) |

## The One Industry Factor That Matters

**The single most important variable:** The virgin-to-recycled plastic price differential.

If recycled plastics consistently command 10-20% premiums over virgin, the industry economics work (barely). Above 30% premium, demand evaporates. Below 0% premium (recycled cheaper than virgin), economics become attractive but this requires either:
1. Sustained high oil prices (making virgin expensive)
2. Mandated recycled content (regulatory forcing)
3. Carbon pricing on virgin plastic production

**What causes it to inflect:**
- Oil price spikes (Ukraine war briefly helped)
- Global petrochemical capacity rationalization (currently oversupplied)
- Binding regulatory mandates with enforcement (EU PPWR implementation)
- Plastic taxes (several EU countries implementing)

**How to monitor:**
- ICIS virgin/recycled price spreads (HDPE, PP, PET)
- Petrochemical capacity utilization rates
- EU PPWR implementation and enforcement timelines

## What Consensus May Be Missing

**Consensus view:** Regulatory mandates will drive demand, technology will mature, economics will improve - this is a "when not if" story.

**What consensus may be missing:**

1. **Technology Risk is Higher Than Acknowledged**
   - 2024-2025 bankruptcy/closure wave suggests pyrolysis technology risk was underestimated
   - Brightmark's 5% capacity utilization after 4 years is a data point, not an anomaly
   - Even "proven" mechanical recycling (PureCycle) struggled to reach 67% uptime

2. **Feedstock is the Binding Constraint, Not Technology**
   - McKinsey: Only 16% of plastic waste collected for recycling globally
   - Collection infrastructure requires $15-20B of the $50B investment need
   - Quality/consistency issues often cited as primary failure mode

3. **Regulatory Mandates May Not Bind**
   - Corporate sustainability retreats already occurring as 2025 deadlines loom
   - EU has history of extending/revising environmental deadlines
   - Enforcement mechanisms for PPWR untested

4. **This Industry May Consolidate to a Few Winners, or May Not Exist at Scale**
   - Multiple technology approaches competing with no clear winner
   - Winner-take-all dynamics unlikely given feedstock/regional constraints
   - Alternative outcome: mechanical recycling + incineration remain dominant; advanced recycling remains niche

**Bullish or bearish for industry participants?**
This is **bearish** for early-stage technology companies like ADUR. The path to commercial revenue is longer and more uncertain than current market commentary suggests. The "winners" - if any emerge - are likely to be large integrated petrochemical companies (Shell, BASF, ExxonMobil) with existing infrastructure, feedstock relationships, and balance sheets to weather the shakeout.

## HCT-Specific Industry Context (Aduro's Technology)

### Claimed Advantages vs. Pyrolysis

Per company disclosures and independent review (Brightlands Chemelot):

| Dimension | Pyrolysis | HCT (Claimed) |
|-----------|-----------|---------------|
| Operating temperature | 400-700C | Lower (unspecified) |
| Contamination tolerance | Low | High |
| Pre-sorting required | Extensive | Minimal |
| Hydrogen required | Often | No |
| Liquid yield | ~78% | ~90% |
| Output boiling range | Higher | Lower (better for crackers) |
| Energy consumption | High | Lower (claimed) |

### November 2025 Milestone

Aduro announced successful pilot-scale steam cracking trials where HCT-derived oil was processed "as is" without hydrotreatment or dilution. This addresses a key pyrolysis weakness (output requiring upgrading) but remains pilot-scale only.

### Critical Questions for HCT

1. **Scale-up risk** - Can pilot results replicate at commercial scale?
2. **Feedstock economics** - Does contamination tolerance translate to lower feedstock costs?
3. **Capital intensity** - What does a commercial HCT facility cost vs. pyrolysis?
4. **Commercial partnerships** - Will Brightlands partnership advance to binding offtake?
