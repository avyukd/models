# Namib Minerals (NAMM): Geopolitical and Sovereign Risk Assessment

## Applicability Check

**Geopolitical risk assessment: HIGHLY MATERIAL.**

Namib Minerals operates gold mines exclusively in Zimbabwe and holds exploration permits in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC). Both jurisdictions rank among the highest-risk mining environments globally. Zimbabwe's president is currently on the OFAC SDN list under Global Magnitsky sanctions. The DRC's eastern provinces are active conflict zones. This analysis is essential.

---

## 2. Sovereign & Regime Risk Assessment

### Zimbabwe

**Political Stability: UNSTABLE / CONTESTED SUCCESSION**

Zimbabwe operates as a de facto one-party state under ZANU-PF, which has governed continuously since independence (1980). The current regime is characterized by:

- **President Emmerson Mnangagwa** is personally sanctioned under OFAC Global Magnitsky (E.O. 13818) as of March 4, 2024, for corruption and serious human rights abuse
- **Vice President Constantino Chiwenga** is also OFAC-designated
- ZANU-PF holds a two-thirds parliamentary majority, enabling constitutional amendments at will
- Active effort to extend Mnangagwa's tenure beyond 2028 constitutional limit (ED2030 campaign)
- Internal factional conflict between Mnangagwa loyalists and Chiwenga faction
- Potential kingmaker: **Kudakwashe Tagwirei** (OFAC-sanctioned businessman), reportedly being positioned for party leadership

**Succession Risk**: The 2028 constitutional deadline creates a binary outcome - either orderly transition (unlikely given history), constitutional manipulation (ongoing), or power struggle. Any of these scenarios introduces policy discontinuity risk.

**Rule of Law: WEAK / SELECTIVE**

- History of fast-track land reform (2000s) with mass expropriation without compensation
- Diamond sector nationalized in 2016 with unilateral cancellation of foreign mining concessions
- Courts have historically failed to enforce rulings against state interests (SADC Tribunal judgment ignored)
- Property rights exist on paper but are subordinate to political expedience
- Recent compensation payments to white farmers (2020 agreement, first payments April 2025) suggest modest improvement but remain incomplete

**History of Intervention Against Foreign Capital**

| Year | Action | Impact |
|------|--------|--------|
| 2000-2013 | Fast-track land reform | Total expropriation of white-owned agricultural land |
| 2008 | Indigenization Act | 51% local ownership required (mining exempted 2020) |
| 2016 | Diamond nationalization | Mining concessions cancelled for 9+ foreign firms |
| 2019 | FX controls | Forced conversion to RTGS dollars at punitive rates |
| 2016-2019 | Fidelity Printers underpayment | $82-132M losses to Metallon from partial/delayed payments |

**Current Attitude Toward Foreign Mining Investment**: Superficially welcoming ("open for business" since 2018), but:
- 2020 amendments permit 100% foreign ownership in mining (positive)
- Minister retains discretion to designate any mineral for 51% indigenization via Statutory Instrument (sword of Damocles)
- New Mines and Minerals Bill (June 2025) introduces additional regulatory uncertainty
- Mandatory gold sales to Fidelity Printers at potentially unfavorable rates

**Likelihood of Adverse Action**: MODERATE-HIGH over 5-10 year horizon
- Succession crisis could trigger policy reversal
- Tagwirei's political ascent introduces direct conflict of interest (he has mining interests)
- Fiscal pressure from economic mismanagement creates incentive for extractive taxation or forced equity transfers

---

### Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC)

**Political Stability: UNSTABLE / ACTIVE CONFLICT**

- President Felix Tshisekedi governs with limited control over eastern provinces
- Eastern DRC (North Kivu, South Kivu, Ituri) is active conflict zone
- M23 rebel group captured Goma (provincial capital) in January 2025
- M23 captured Bukavu (South Kivu capital) in February 2025
- Estimated 4,000-7,000 Rwandan troops fighting alongside M23
- 7.3 million internally displaced persons (one of world's largest)

**Rule of Law: EXTREMELY WEAK**

- 450+ illegal Chinese mining operations discovered in South Kivu alone (2024)
- Armed groups control multiple mining sites and extract $140M+ annually from gold in Ituri alone
- Nearly all artisanally-mined gold is traded illegally
- Mining permits subject to corruption, political influence, and security dynamics

**Mining Code Changes (2018 onwards)**

| Provision | Implication |
|-----------|-------------|
| 10% free carry to state | Mandatory equity dilution |
| +5% at each renewal | Increasing state ownership over time |
| 10% Congolese natural person ownership | Local partner requirement |
| 50% for processing operations | Severe constraint on vertical integration |
| Strategic minerals: 10% royalty | Cobalt/coltan; gold currently ~3.5% |
| 50% excess profits tax | Triggered when prices exceed feasibility study by 25% |
| Stability period reduced 10 to 5 years | Shorter protection from legislative changes |
| 60-100% repatriation requirement | Limited ability to retain earnings offshore |

**Security Situation for Gold Exploration**

Namib's 13 DRC exploration permits face:
- Proximity to active conflict zones (specific location unclear from public filings)
- Armed group presence in gold-producing areas
- History of site seizures (e.g., Twangiza Mining lost to M23, ~$70M gold stolen)
- Physical security costs prohibitive for junior exploration
- Conflict minerals compliance requirements (Dodd-Frank Section 1502, EU regulations)

**Likelihood of Adverse Action**: VERY HIGH
- Permits could be revoked, renegotiated, or become inaccessible due to conflict
- Any discovery would immediately attract state/military/rebel attention
- Converting exploration to production in current environment is extremely difficult

## 3. Regulatory & State Intervention Risk

### Zimbabwe: Mechanisms of State Extraction

**Forced Divestment Risk**: LOW currently, but CONTINGENT
- 2020 indigenization law amendments permit 100% foreign ownership
- However, Minister can designate any mineral for 51% indigenization via Statutory Instrument
- No current designation, but risk re-emerges with succession crisis or fiscal pressure

**License/Permit Risk**: MODERATE
- Mines and Minerals Act Section 324 permits expropriation of mining locations deemed "inadequately worked"
- New Mines and Minerals Bill (June 2025) introduces additional uncertainty
- Artisanal miner encroachment at Mazowe and Redwing creates de facto operational challenge

**Mandatory Offtake Risk**: HIGH (STRUCTURAL)
- All gold must be sold to Fidelity Printers and Refiners (state entity)
- Historical precedent of underpayment and delayed settlement (Metallon lost $82-132M)
- No alternative buyer legally permitted
- This is the single most reliable mechanism of state value extraction

**Royalties and Taxation**: MODERATE
- Current royalty rates are manageable
- Finance (No. 2) Act 2024 introduces special capital gains tax on mining title transfers
- Fiscal pressure creates incentive for incremental tax increases

**Export/Currency Restrictions**: HIGH
- 20% mandatory surrender of export proceeds for miners (converted at potentially unfavorable rates)
- ZiG currency has lost 50%+ of value in first six months (April-October 2024)
- Plan to make ZiG sole legal tender by 2030 would eliminate USD pricing
- 3% cap on management fees/royalties limits ability to extract value

### DRC: Mechanisms of State Extraction

**Mandatory State Equity**: CERTAIN
- 10% free carry at permit grant
- +5% at each renewal
- 10% to Congolese natural persons
- Net effect: 25%+ of any project ceded to state/local interests before production

**Export Suspensions**: PRECEDENT SET
- February 2025: 4-month cobalt export ban enacted
- Similar measures could be applied to gold under "strategic mineral" designation

**Permit Revocation**: HIGH RISK
- Permits require ongoing compliance with payment schedules, work programs, and social commitments
- Failure to advance (common in junior exploration) creates revocation risk
- Political/military dynamics can override legal frameworks

## 4. Conflict & Sanctions Exposure

### OFAC Sanctions: The Core Compliance Challenge

**Current Status**:
- Zimbabwe country-specific sanctions terminated March 4, 2024 (E.O. 13288/13391/13469 revoked)
- HOWEVER: Key figures designated under Global Magnitsky (E.O. 13818):
  - President Emmerson Mnangagwa
  - Vice President Constantino Chiwenga
  - First Lady Auxillia Mnangagwa
  - Kudakwashe Tagwirei (ZANU-PF-connected businessman, mining interests)
  - Multiple officials and entities in gold smuggling network (December 2024)

**Implications for Namib Minerals**:

1. **No Broad Sector Sanctions**: Operating in Zimbabwe mining is not per se prohibited for US persons/entities

2. **SDN Screening Required**: Any transaction with designated individuals/entities is prohibited. This includes:
   - Government officials who are designated
   - Entities 50%+ owned by designated persons
   - Any commercial relationship with Tagwirei-linked businesses

3. **Gold Supply Chain Risk**: December 2024 designations targeted global gold smuggling network operating in Zimbabwe. Namib must ensure its gold supply chain does not intersect with sanctioned networks.

4. **Reputational Risk**: US-listed company operating in country whose president is OFAC-designated for corruption and human rights abuse creates investor relations challenge.

**Secondary Sanctions Exposure**: LIMITED
- Zimbabwe is not subject to comprehensive sanctions like Russia/Iran/North Korea
- No significant risk of secondary sanctions for operating in Zimbabwe

### DRC Conflict Minerals

- Gold is a designated conflict mineral under Dodd-Frank Section 1502
- SEC reporting requirements apply to US-listed issuers
- Any DRC-sourced gold must comply with supply chain due diligence (OECD guidelines)
- Eastern DRC gold is presumptively high-risk due to armed group involvement

## 5. Currency & Capital Controls

### Zimbabwe

**ZiG Currency (introduced April 2024)**:
- Claimed gold backing ($900M in reserves)
- Lost 50% of official value in first 6 months
- Parallel market discount of 75%+ observed
- Inflation: 2.5% (Dec 2024) to 14.6% (Jan 2025)
- Plan to phase out USD by 2030 introduces long-term pricing risk

**Export Surrender Requirements**:
- Miners: 20% of forex earnings surrendered to RBZ
- Converted at official rate (disadvantageous vs. parallel market)
- Remainder (70%) retained, but ZiG equivalent of 5% into USDDDF
- Net effect: ~25-30% of export value subject to currency risk

**Historical Precedent**:
- Metallon lost $82-132M from forced ZWD conversions at punitive rates (2016-2019)
- Current ZiG regime follows similar mechanics
- Risk of policy tightening during succession crisis or fiscal stress

**Repatriation Risk**: MODERATE
- Bilateral investment treaties exist but enforcement is weak
- ZIDA theoretically facilitates repatriation but RBZ controls forex allocation

### DRC

**Repatriation Requirements**:
- 60% during investment return phase
- 100% thereafter
- Limits ability to accumulate offshore capital

**Currency**: Congolese Franc, volatile but less restrictive than Zimbabwe
- Dollarization common in mining sector
- Forex availability constrained outside major cities

## 6. Scenario & Tail Risk Framing

### Base Case: Status Quo Continuation

- How Mine continues producing 30-40 koz annually
- Fidelity Printers pays at acceptable rates
- Mazowe/Redwing restart progresses slowly (artisanal miner challenges)
- DRC permits remain dormant (no significant exploration expenditure)
- Mnangagwa succession resolved without major disruption
- Modest erosion of margins from ZiG depreciation and incremental taxation

**Impact**: Slow, grinding value erosion; mediocre returns but no capital loss event

### Adverse Case: Policy Shift or Escalation

Triggers:
- Mnangagwa succession crisis (2028 or earlier)
- Fiscal crisis prompts indigenization revival
- New Mining Act introduces adverse provisions
- Artisanal miner conflict escalates at Mazowe/Redwing

Manifestations:
- 51% indigenization requirement imposed on gold
- Export surrender increased to 50%+
- Fidelity Printers returns to systematic underpayment
- Production disruption from artisanal miner violence

**Impact**: 30-50% impairment of Zimbabwe asset value; DRC permits written off

### Severe Tail Case: Expropriation or Conflict

Triggers:
- Full-scale succession conflict in Zimbabwe
- Tagwirei faction seizes assets for personal network
- DRC conflict expands to southern mining regions
- US adds Zimbabwe to comprehensive sanctions

Manifestations:
- Mining licenses revoked or transferred to connected parties
- Foreign shareholder rights extinguished (diamond sector precedent)
- Physical loss of DRC assets to armed groups
- Unable to operate as US-listed company in Zimbabwe

**Impact**: Near-total loss of invested capital (80-100%)

### Realistic Assessment

NAMM has NO meaningful mitigants against sovereign risk. It operates with:
- 100% concentration in one of the world's highest-risk jurisdictions
- Single producing asset (catastrophic if disrupted)
- No credible enforcement mechanism for shareholder rights
- Pre-revenue DRC assets that add risk without diversification

## The One Geopolitical Risk That Matters

**The single most important geopolitical factor for this investment is Zimbabwe's post-Mnangagwa succession.**

The ZANU-PF regime is attempting to extend Mnangagwa's tenure to 2030, but the 2028 constitutional deadline creates a binary forcing function. The succession dynamics involve:

1. **Mnangagwa faction** (including OFAC-sanctioned Tagwirei) seeking to retain power
2. **Chiwenga faction** (Vice President, also OFAC-sanctioned) with military backing
3. **No legitimate opposition** capable of challenging ZANU-PF

**Trigger**: Constitutional manipulation fails, health event, or intra-party conflict erupts before/after 2028.

**Impact**: Succession crises in Zimbabwe have historically produced policy discontinuity, economic disruption, and targeting of foreign capital. The fast-track land reform began during a period of regime instability. Diamond nationalization occurred as Mugabe consolidated power. A contested succession could produce:
- Revival of indigenization policies
- Asset seizures by connected factions
- Currency crisis and capital controls
- Operational disruption from instability

This risk is binary and largely unhedgeable. The probability distribution is fat-tailed: most outcomes are manageable, but tail outcomes involve near-total loss.

## What Consensus May Be Missing

**Non-Consensus Insight: The market may be UNDER-pricing the Fidelity Printers structural extraction mechanism while OVER-pricing headline political risk.**

The typical Zimbabwe risk narrative focuses on:
- Sanctions (largely terminated)
- Indigenization (currently suspended)
- Expropriation (possible but not imminent)

What this misses:

**Fidelity Printers and Refiners is the more reliable value extraction mechanism.** This state-owned entity is the mandatory buyer of all gold produced in Zimbabwe. Historical precedent (Metallon: $82-132M losses) shows the state can extract value continuously through:
- Delayed payments
- Below-market conversion rates
- Forced ZiG/local currency settlement
- Partial payment during forex shortages

This is not a binary risk - it's a continuous drag on economics that is difficult to detect from outside the country and impossible to hedge.

**Implication**: Even if no dramatic expropriation occurs, the structural mechanics of mandatory offtake to a state entity with aligned incentives to underpay represent a slow, continuous value leak. Investors focused on headline political risk may miss this grinding erosion.

**Second Non-Consensus Observation**: The DRC exploration permits are likely worthless in practice.

The market may be assigning some optionality value to 13 DRC exploration permits. In reality:
- Eastern DRC is in active military conflict (M23 captured major cities in 2025)
- Converting exploration permits to producing mines requires security guarantees that don't exist
- Conflict minerals compliance creates additional barriers
- Any discovery would immediately attract armed group attention

These should be valued at zero for investment purposes.

## Final Classification

**Geopolitical Risk Level:** HIGH

**Primary Risk Vector:** Sovereign extraction via mandatory offtake (Fidelity Printers) and succession-driven policy discontinuity

**Tail Risk Severity:** EXISTENTIAL

Permanent capital loss scenarios are plausible within normal planning horizons. The succession crisis (2028) provides a specific trigger point, but adverse policy shifts could occur earlier.

**Key Monitoring Signals:**
1. ED2030 constitutional amendment progress (higher risk if passed)
2. Tagwirei's political positioning (if elevated, direct conflict of interest)
3. Fidelity Printers payment terms and settlement timeliness

## Conclusion

Namib Minerals presents a case study in concentrated sovereign risk. The company operates in two of the world's highest-risk mining jurisdictions with:
- 100% revenue concentration in Zimbabwe
- A single producing asset (How Mine)
- A country whose president and key officials are OFAC-designated
- Assets acquired from receivership caused by state underpayment
- Pre-revenue DRC assets in active conflict zones

The question is not whether geopolitical risk is material - it is whether the risk is appropriately priced. If valuation assumes stable production, reliable offtake, and eventual mine restarts without policy interference, the market is likely under-pricing tail risk. If valuation already reflects a substantial discount for jurisdiction, the grinding Fidelity Printers extraction mechanism may still be underweighted.

**Can state action overwhelm the business economics?** Yes. Historical precedent (diamond nationalization, land reform, Metallon underpayment) demonstrates Zimbabwe's willingness to extract value from or expropriate foreign-owned assets.

**Is this risk mispriced, unavoidable, or unacceptable?** Likely mispriced given the specific OFAC status of regime principals and structural extraction mechanisms. Unavoidable without geographic diversification. Whether it is unacceptable depends on return expectations and loss tolerance.

**Does this belong in the too-hard pile regardless of valuation?** For investors without specialized emerging market expertise and loss tolerance, yes. The combination of binary succession risk, structural offtake extraction, OFAC-designated counterparties, and zero meaningful mitigants creates a risk profile that may be inappropriate for most portfolios regardless of apparent cheapness.
