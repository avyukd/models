## 2. KEY DRIVERS (The 1-3 Things That Matter)

**1. Loan Portfolio Credit Performance** - This is the load-bearing variable. LEGH has transformed into a captive finance company serving subprime borrowers. The $200M+ loan portfolio has never been tested through a recession. Delinquency rates, charge-offs, and recovery rates in a downturn will determine whether this business model survives or implodes.

**2. Interest Income Trajectory** - At $41M and growing 11% YoY, financing income is now the primary profit driver. The sustainability of this growth depends on origination volumes, credit quality, and the competitive response from Berkshire's dominant financing arms.

- **LEGH is a subprime lender disguised as a manufactured housing company.** The market may be valuing it on housing comps (CVCO, SKY) when the correct framework is consumer finance with attached manufacturing.

- **Structural industry tailwinds are real but financing-constrained.** Housing affordability favors manufactured housing, but Berkshire's 75% control of chattel lending limits LEGH's growth runway.

## 5. THE SETUP

**Business Reality:**
Legacy Housing is a vertically integrated manufactured housing company that builds, sells (through company-owned retail), and finances homes. The critical insight is that the financing business has become the dominant profit driver. In 2024, interest revenue of $41.2M contributed disproportionately to net income given the capital-light nature of manufacturing.

**Why This Matters:**
The company serves credit-constrained buyers who cannot access traditional mortgages. This is subprime lending by another name. The loans are chattel (personal property, not real estate), meaning no mortgage protections and higher default risk in stress.

**Industry Structure:**
Manufactured housing is a consolidated oligopoly with Clayton Homes (Berkshire) controlling ~50% of production and ~75% of financing through 21st Mortgage and Vanderbilt. LEGH has ~3% share. The industry is supply-constrained by FINANCING availability, not demand. This means LEGH's growth is tethered to its ability to expand its captive book - a strategy that compounds credit risk.

**Recent Developments:**
October 2025 saw the departure of CEO Bates, CFO, and General Counsel following a year-long strategic review. Founders are back in control. November 2025 brought the AmeriCasa acquisition - the first significant M&A in company history. These changes could signal either distress or a return to conservative discipline. We lack visibility to determine which.

## 8. RISKS & KILL SWITCHES

**Primary Risk: Credit Cycle Exposure**
The loan portfolio is entirely untested. In a recession, subprime chattel loans to credit-constrained buyers could experience 20-40% losses (based on historical MH finance data from 2008-2010). A 25% loss rate on $200M would be a $50M hit - wiping nearly a year's earnings.

**Secondary Risk: Competitive Encirclement**
Clayton/Berkshire controls the industry. They could tighten financing terms, cut prices, or simply outgrow LEGH into irrelevance.

**Structural Risk: Model Dependency**
Without captive financing, LEGH cannot sell to its target customer. The manufacturing and financing businesses are co-dependent. Problems in one destroy both.

**Kill Switches (What Invalidates Any Thesis):**
- Delinquency rates exceed 10%
- Charge-offs exceed 5% annually
- Recession-driven volume collapse
- Loss of key manufacturing capacity
- Regulatory intervention in chattel lending

## 9. WHY NEUTRAL (TOO HARD)

**The Core Problem:**
I cannot underwrite the credit risk. The loan portfolio has only existed in benign economic conditions. There is no empirical basis to estimate losses in a downturn. This makes normalized earnings impossible to calculate with confidence.

**What Would Need to Be True for a BUY:**
- Demonstrated credit performance through an economic cycle
- Clear evidence of conservative underwriting standards
- Visibility into loss reserves and recovery rates
- Compelling margin of safety accounting for credit risk

**What Would Need to Be True for a SELL/SHORT:**
- Evidence of deteriorating credit quality
- Aggressive underwriting standards
- Rising delinquencies without adequate provisioning
- Overvaluation relative to risk-adjusted earnings

**Current State:**
We have neither. The loan book is a black box. Management has changed. The economic cycle is uncertain. Valuation looks cheap on reported earnings but may be expensive on risk-adjusted, through-cycle earnings.

**The Honest Assessment:**
This could be a 2x if credit holds and the market re-rates it as a quality finance company. It could be a 50% loss if a recession reveals the loan book is impaired. I cannot assign probabilities to these outcomes with conviction.

**Assumption Density: HIGH**
Any thesis requires assuming credit losses stay low. This is unverifiable.

**Execution Dependency: HIGH**
Founder return must translate to conservative discipline. This is unverifiable.

**Sensitivity to Unknowns: EXTREME**
A 10% vs 30% credit loss rate in recession is the difference between a great investment and a disaster.

---

## 10. WHAT MOVES THIS OUT OF THE TOO HARD PILE

1. **A recession that tests the loan book.** Paradoxically, a mild recession with manageable losses would be the best catalyst - it would provide empirical evidence of credit quality.

2. **Disclosure improvements.** Detailed loan-level data, vintage analysis, delinquency breakdowns, and reserve methodology would allow underwriting the credit risk.

3. **Third-party validation.** A securitization or warehouse facility with a major bank would provide external credit validation.

4. **Time.** Another 2-3 years of clean credit performance through varied economic conditions would build confidence.

## 11. PORTFOLIO IMPLICATIONS

**Position Size: ZERO**

Not because the opportunity is bad, but because the risk is unquantifiable. Position sizing requires understanding downside magnitude. Here, the downside is "somewhere between 20% and 70% depending on credit losses we cannot estimate."

**If Forced to Take a View:**
Gun to head, I would lean slightly bullish. The founder alignment, zero debt, and structural housing tailwinds are real. But I cannot responsibly size a position when the central risk factor is unobservable.

**What I Would Do Instead:**
- Monitor quarterly credit metrics
- Wait for a recession to test the book
- Revisit if/when credit quality becomes observable
- Consider a small position if price drops 30%+ creating larger margin of safety

---

## 12. CONCLUSION

Legacy Housing presents an interesting setup - cheap valuation, aligned management, structural tailwinds, and a differentiated business model. However, the company has transformed into a subprime lender, and the loan portfolio has never been stress-tested.

The market may be wrong in viewing this as a housing company. But I cannot determine if it's wrong-cheap or wrong-expensive without understanding the credit risk. That makes this a pass.

**Final Decision: NEUTRAL (Too Hard)**

The variant perception exists. The setup is interesting. But the central variable - credit performance in a downturn - is unobservable. When the load-bearing assumption in any thesis is "credit losses stay low" and there is no empirical basis to assess that assumption, the responsible decision is to pass.

This is not a rejection of the opportunity. It is an admission that I cannot underwrite it with the information available. If credit quality becomes observable through stress or disclosure, this deserves a fresh look.
