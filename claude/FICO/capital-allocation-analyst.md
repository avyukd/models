# FICO (Fair Isaac Corporation) -- Management & Capital Allocation Analysis

**Date:** 2026-02-24 | **Price:** ~$1,200 | **Shares Outstanding:** ~24M diluted

---

## 1. Management Overview

Will Lansing has served as CEO since January 2012 -- over 14 years. He owns ~371,000 shares (direct + trust), roughly 1.5% of shares outstanding, worth ~$445M at current prices. Total compensation was $36M in FY2025 (per 2025 proxy). Under his tenure, TSR exceeds 5,300%.

FICO has no meaningful M&A since the early 2010s (Entiera, Adeptra). The company divested its Collection & Recovery business to Constellation Software. Management has essentially run FICO as a capital-light, single-product cash machine focused on the FICO Score monopoly and its Scores/Software segments.

## 2. Incentive Structure & Alignment

Per the 2025 DEF 14A, compensation is weighted toward performance-based cash and equity tied to adjusted revenue, adjusted EBITDA, and relative TSR vs. the S&P 500.

**What behavior does this encourage?** Revenue growth and margin expansion -- both of which FICO has delivered. The TSR metric is a double-edged sword: it rewards stock price appreciation, which can incentivize leverage and buybacks that boost EPS mechanically.

**Concern:** Lansing has made 238 sales and zero purchases over a recent six-month period. While this is common for long-tenured CEOs with concentrated positions, it is a net seller pattern. His $445M stake provides meaningful alignment, but it was built through grants, not open-market purchases. The alignment is structural (accumulated equity) rather than conviction-driven (buying with personal capital).

## 3. Stock-Based Compensation Discipline

|-----|---------|------|-----------|------|-----------|
| 2021 | $1.32B | $113M | 8.5% | $416M | 27.1% |
| 2022 | $1.38B | $115M | 8.4% | $503M | 22.9% |
| 2023 | $1.51B | $124M | 8.2% | $459M | 27.0% |
| 2024 | $1.72B | $149M | 8.7% | $624M | 23.9% |
| 2025 | $1.99B | $157M | 7.9% | $770M | 20.4% |

SBC as a percentage of revenue has held steady at ~8%, which is moderate for software but not cheap. As a percentage of FCF, SBC has actually improved from 27% to 20% as cash generation scaled faster. The diluted share count has declined from ~29M (FY2019) to ~24M (FY2025), a genuine ~17% reduction. This is real shrinkage, not SBC-offset buybacks masquerading as returns. Net dilution risk is low.

## 4. Capital Allocation Track Record

### a) Buyback History and Discipline

This is the central question. FICO has repurchased $5.2B in stock over FY2021-2025:

| FY | Buybacks | Approx Avg Price Paid | Price Today |
|----|----------|-----------------------|-------------|
| 2021 | $966M | ~$450-550 | $1,200 |
| 2022 | $1.16B | ~$450-550 | $1,200 |
| 2023 | $482M | ~$700-900 | $1,200 |
| 2024 | $961M | ~$1,200-1,500 | $1,200 |
| 2025 | $1.62B | ~$1,600-2,000 | $1,200 |

The FY2021-2022 buybacks were excellent -- shares purchased at $450-550 have more than doubled. FY2023 buybacks were solid. But FY2024-2025 buybacks totaling $2.6B were done at prices between $1,200-2,000+, and the stock currently trades at $1,200. The most recent and largest tranche of buybacks ($1.62B in FY2025) appears to have been executed near all-time highs. This is the classic pattern: disciplined when cheap, aggressive when expensive.

### b) Debt-Funded Buybacks

Long-term debt: $1.0B (FY2021) to $2.8B (FY2025) -- a $1.8B increase. Total debt is $3.2B against $134M cash. Shareholders' equity is negative $1.8B (driven by $7.7B cumulative treasury stock).

Interest coverage remains adequate at ~6.8x EBIT, and FCF of $770M comfortably services the debt. But the trajectory is aggressive: debt nearly tripled in four years to fund buybacks. FICO borrowed $1.8B to buy back stock at increasingly elevated prices.

**Is this sustainable?** At current FCF levels, yes -- for now. But FICO is a cyclical-adjacent business (mortgage volumes, lending activity). A credit contraction that reduced Scores revenue would compress FCF while the debt remains fixed. The margin of safety has narrowed.

### c) M&A

Effectively zero meaningful M&A in a decade. This is a positive signal -- management has not empire-built. The divestiture to Constellation Software suggests willingness to prune.

### d) Reinvestment

FICO's reinvestment needs are minimal. Capex is under $10M annually. The business is a toll booth on consumer credit, requiring little incremental capital. This is what makes the capital allocation decision so binary: almost all FCF goes to buybacks and debt service.

## 5. Learning vs. Repetition

Management showed discipline in FY2021-2022, buying back stock aggressively at reasonable prices. But FY2025's $1.62B in repurchases at peak prices -- funded partly with debt -- suggests the buyback program has become mechanical rather than price-sensitive. The board authorized a new $1B program in June 2025 even as the stock traded above $2,000. This is repetition of a formula (lever up, buy back) without apparent price discipline.

## 6. Forward-Looking Capital Allocation Risk

FICO will likely continue aggressive buybacks regardless of price. The incentive structure rewards EPS growth and TSR -- buybacks mechanically boost both. With $3.2B in debt and negative equity, further leveraging to fund buybacks at elevated multiples would compound the risk. If Scores revenue declines in a credit downturn, the fixed debt burden becomes a real constraint.

---

## The One Thing That Determines Value Creation

**Whether management treats buybacks as price-sensitive capital allocation or as a mechanical EPS lever.** The FY2025 data suggests the latter. When a company borrows $1.8B over four years to buy back stock at prices 3-4x where it started the period, the discipline question is live. If FICO continues buying at 30-40x FCF, buybacks shift from value-creating to value-neutral or destructive.

## What Consensus May Be Missing

Consensus treats FICO as a "great capital allocator" based on the cumulative track record -- and the early buybacks were genuinely excellent. But the marginal dollar of buybacks has gotten progressively worse. The FY2025 repurchases ($1.62B at ~$1,600-2,000 avg) may prove value-destructive, and they were partially debt-funded. The market gives full credit for the buyback program without distinguishing between cheap and expensive repurchases. The 2021 buybacks and the 2025 buybacks are not the same decision, but they are treated as evidence of the same discipline.

---

## Final Classification

- **Incentive Alignment:** Mixed -- large ownership but grant-derived, consistent selling, incentives favor EPS/TSR mechanics over ROIC
- **Capital Allocation Discipline:** Moderate -- excellent early track record, deteriorating price discipline at scale
- **Dilution Risk:** Low -- genuine net share reduction of ~17% over five years
- **Primary Risk:** Continued debt-funded buybacks at elevated prices during a cyclical peak in credit activity, with no price sensitivity in the repurchase framework
